# Synthesis notes B — interviews 4–7

These notes connect the accounts of Tiago, Inês, Duarte and Sofia to themes, counterexamples and follow-up questions. Quotations refer to the interview transcripts.

## Tiago — interview 4

**Participant profile:** 16; lives in Famões, Odivelas, with mother and younger sister; second-year restaurant/hospitality student at a vocational school near Areeiro; roughly one-hour commute each way using a bus and metro; some weekend pastelaria shifts. He reports no current clubs and says he stopped futsal because of work and travel. Source: `interview_4_tiago.md`, participant summary and transcript.

**Three exact participant excerpts:**

- “I'm a visitor. A visitor who comes every day.” — `interview_4_tiago.md:129`.
- “Not like I don't want to, I can't.” — `interview_4_tiago.md:143`.
- “I don't need an app, I don't need a project. Just stop the bus.” — `interview_4_tiago.md:179`.

**Interview themes:** He walks backwards one bus stop to board before the bus fills, shares arrival reports in an eight-person chat, and doubts the official app. One complaint received an opaque referral to an operator; he did not repeat it. Living and studying in different municipalities makes responsibility and eligibility unclear to him. His principal demand is functioning transport; the account does not justify reinterpreting all frustration as a desire for civic activity.

**Contradictions / counterexamples:** He first denies formal reporting or being consulted, then recalls a complaint and a school survey. A public implementation deadline sounds reassuring, but he admits he probably would not follow it up personally. He initially says he is not unsafe, then describes a breakdown that left people walking beside traffic in rain. Interest does not remove his lack of time.

**Prompt effects:** Class-time participation, hands-on construction, a crossing, and benches are responses to the explicitly supplied €5,000/six-week school-front scenario (lines 135–149). Preference for a challenge follows a loaded council-versus-short-project-with-friends choice (lines 163–169). He still rejects an after-school challenge and asks whether it counts for a grade. Neither response demonstrates spontaneous demand for the proposed programme.

## Inês — interview 5

**Participant profile:** 16; 11th-year science and technology student at a public secondary school in Benfica; manual wheelchair user with spina bifida; uses buses, a prebooked adapted service, and lifts from her mother. Swims, uses an informal wheelchair-user chat, previously joined Parlamento dos Jovens, and is not in an association. She is not Sofia's daughter, who is a different person called Inês, aged 15.

**Three exact participant excerpts:**

- “Like I was decoration. Like they needed a wheelchair in the photo, and I'm a wheelchair.” — `interview_5_ines.md:95`.
- “If the challenge is "go around the neighbourhood and take photos of problems", I can't go everywhere my friends go.” — `interview_5_ines.md:133`.
- “Those are also my problems. I'm a teenager first.” — `interview_5_ines.md:151`.

**Interview themes:** A journey depends on several access conditions working together; informal lift reports and a helpful driver reduce uncertainty. A previous route-mapping exercise produced publicity but little visible change in the narrative. Access to participation itself requires a suitable building, route, timing, and return journey. She wants to contribute as a whole person with opinions about seating and night buses, rather than being assigned the sole role of accessibility representative. Failed transport also costs her mother work time.

**Contradictions / counterexamples:** Her frustration with token consultation does not eliminate willingness to contribute: she says she would make the map again because otherwise kerbs may be ignored. She wants not to be reduced to disability, while her first project suggestion addresses disabled parking; that is compatible with having multiple interests, not evidence of inconsistency to correct. She might vote for somebody else's bike rack. Her mother both encouraged independent travel and monitors her location. These details challenge a single universal narrative of disengagement or overprotective parents.

**Prompt effects:** Her parking proposal and conditional agreement follow the supplied €5,000/six-week scenario (lines 115–121). Her choice of team challenge follows the loaded comparison with a council meeting (lines 129–135). Her immediately stated access objection is a useful failure scenario for testing any field activity; uptake still needs to be checked in a properly accessible activity. Dates, a named contact, and proof of prior implementation are elicited by a question asking what would make her trust the winning proposal (lines 123–127).

The source does not establish actual legal eligibility or scooter rules.

**Three exact participant excerpts:**

- “it feels like writing a letter to nobody.” — `interview_6_duarte.md:96` (exact lowercase excerpt).
- “Because nobody's in charge. Like, nobody says "meeting at 4."” — `interview_6_duarte.md:112`.
- “If it's just for fun, I'd say yes and then not go.” — `interview_6_duarte.md:158`.

**Interview themes:** He has a near-miss video but posts it only socially; he sees no clear official destination or feedback. His social group offers flexible attendance and visible outputs. He has a skill he could contribute—filming and editing—without wanting to write proposals. A short trip's independence matters to him, while school-front obstructions lead him onto the pavement.

**Contradictions / counterexamples:** He condemns drivers' behaviour while admitting speed, pavement riding, and little helmet use. He cares about physical changes yet expects to miss a voluntary project unless it fits exams, friends, and school credit. Location tracking does not tell his mother how he travels. Therefore visible impact alone may not yield sustained participation; incentives and availability remain rival explanations.

**Prompt effects:** The €5,000/six-week prompt supplies the budget, time frame, and school location before he proposes bollards and filming (lines 132–142). The council-versus-challenge question supplies the attractive social context before he chooses it (lines 152–162). His existing video-making is distinct from his hypothetical willingness to film a project; follow-up uptake should test that commitment.

**Participant profile:** 46; nurse with rotating shifts at Santa Maria; lives in Lumiar; children Inês, 15, in secondary school near Alvalade, and Tomás, 11, in basic school in Lumiar. Drives the morning trips; her daughter recently started returning by metro. A parents' association member who rarely attends. Her younger child falls outside the project target age; Sofia is a stakeholder, not a seventh teen.

**Three exact participant excerpts:**

- “Not no to the idea. No to her schedule.” — `interview_7_sofia_parent.md:84`.
- “And actually they fixed it, I'd say in about two weeks.” — `interview_7_sofia_parent.md:68` (her reported lamp repair).
- “It's not that I don't trust her. I don't trust the street.” — `interview_7_sofia_parent.md:104`.

**Interview themes:** Her daughter's permission to travel changed after another parent suggested travelling together. Her fear concerns the last walking segment, darkness, and harassment; she has not fully discussed the reason with her daughter. A reachable Junta contact and an apparently successful lamp repair distinguish institutions within the account. Shift work limits her involvement. Teen participation has a family scheduling and transport dimension.

**Contradictions / counterexamples:** She supports autonomy and youth voice but decides trips and watches location sharing; she acknowledges contributing to school-gate double parking. Her scooter prohibition coexists with choosing not to investigate a suspected breach. The successful lamp repair is a counterexample to “every official report is ignored.” Support for a project does not mean she can organise it or that her daughter wants it; this is a parent perspective.

**Prompt effects:** Conditional support for a school-time project, upfront funding, a deadline, and a named official all follow the explicitly supplied €5,000/six-week scenario or its credibility follow-up (lines 82–96). The transcript contains no direct council-versus-challenge comparison for this parent. Her temporary-lighting and school-street suggestions follow a prompt for one-afternoon testing, not an unprompted project demand.

## Implications to carry into the synthesis

1. **Do not collapse physical barriers into motivation.** Tiago asks for a reliable bus; Inês asks to arrive independently; Sofia's permission depends on the street. A participation intervention may fail if it adds effort without addressing those constraints.
2. **Treat school time, accessibility, jurisdiction, and parental logistics as separate questions.** A fun format is not an answer to all four. A school-front scope may also miss Tiago's worst problem on the home side of his journey.
3. **Avoid treating “with friends” as universally inclusive.** These four interviews describe existing peer networks and do not contain an isolated teen. That leaves isolation an untested gap, not a finding. Friendship recruitment and teams could exclude someone without an existing group; investigate this with actual users.
4. **Do not claim majority-vote fairness.** Inês's willingness to vote for another project does not show that voting protects minority access needs; the transcripts have not tested that tradeoff.
5. **Separate accounts of existing behaviour from elicited concept reactions.** The latter answer prompts about the already proposed solution. The prompts bundle money, speed, friends, and real change, preventing attribution to any single feature.
6. **Preserve counterexamples.** Inês remains willing despite disappointment; Sofia describes a successful local response; Duarte wants grades/CV value; Tiago cannot attend even an appealing challenge. These keep insights conditional.
7. **No inferential counts.** Use the interviews to explain differences and mechanisms. Broader prevalence questions would require a different sampling and measurement approach.
