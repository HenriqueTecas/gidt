# Methodology review: interview research

## Report-ready methodology

The team combined desk research with seven semi-structured interviews: six teenagers and one parent. The participants differ in mobility habits, prior civic involvement, accessibility needs and parental dependence. Analysis grouped accounts into themes, separated interview evidence from interpretation, retained counterexamples, and developed an empathy map, insights, a point of view and HMWs.

Questions about recent journeys and previous participation attempts are distinguished from reactions to the proposed €5,000 project, six-week deadline and team challenge. The latter are prompted concept reactions rather than spontaneously requested features.

## Analysis considerations

- **Concept framing:** keep the earlier school-based audit, micro-budget and street-lab idea open to revision in light of participant accounts.
- **Leading comparisons:** the council-versus-challenge prompt changes duration, social context and format at once. Compare equally specified options and vary one feature at a time in follow-up work.
- **Anchoring:** the interviewer introduces the budget, six weeks and winning idea. Check actual funding and delivery constraints with the responsible institution.
- **Intention and attendance:** willingness expressed in an interview should be followed up through voluntary uptake and return.
- **Differences between participants:** preserve distinct motivations, constraints and counterexamples instead of collapsing them into a universal persona.
- **Multiple priorities:** Inês can want accessible streets and support a bike rack for classmates. These interests are compatible.
- **External claims:** resolve the desk report's source references before using institutional facts, policies or numerical claims.
- **Separate accounts:** Sofia's daughter Inês, 15, is a different person from interview 5's Inês, 16.

## Follow-up plan

The following activities extend the interview research by checking behaviour, comparing alternatives and examining feasibility.

| # | Assumption from the original report | Non-leading question or real-world test | Signal that would challenge it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Teenagers distinguish participating from being surveyed. | “Tell me about the last time someone asked for your view on something that affected you. What happened next?” Ask how they would describe their own role before introducing categories such as consultation or influence. | Teenagers regard giving an opinion as meaningful participation even without a visible decision, or do not make the proposed distinction. |
| 2 | Perceived impact matters more than rewards. | “Tell me about something optional you chose to do recently. What made it worth doing?” After open exploration, compare otherwise similar, honestly described activities differing in impact or recognition; include a decline option. | Participation persists mainly for skills, grades, prizes or friends, while evidence of impact makes little difference to the choice. |
| 3 | Mobility is important enough to compete with other commitments. | Map the person's actual previous week. Ask which local issues, if any, they spent time acting on and what they would have to give up to join a specific feasible activity. Observe voluntary uptake later. | Mobility is frustrating but consistently ranks below other priorities; no workable commitment emerges even when interest is expressed. |
| 4 | School is a convenient and credible participation channel. | “Where did you last hear about an activity you chose to join? What made you act on it?” Ask separately about experiences of school-organised optional activities; compare the same real invitation through school and another familiar channel where feasible. | School framing is associated with compulsory work, embarrassment or distrust, or only reaches pupils already involved in student organisations. |
| 5 | Parents gatekeep mobility and participation. | Ask teenagers and parents separately about a recent trip or activity that went ahead or did not: who decided, what changed the decision and what practical arrangements were needed? | Teenagers decide independently, permission is routine, or time, cost and access explain non-attendance better than parental approval. |
| 6 | A different format will engage previously disengaged teenagers. | First establish previous participation through concrete examples. Offer a small voluntary activity with accessible timing and a genuine option to decline; track attendance and return without treating an interview promise as uptake. | The same already active pupils participate, while previous non-participants still decline or do not return despite the changed format. |
| 7 | Visual and hands-on formats reduce participation barriers. | Let teens explain the same personally relevant issue using their choice of conversation, text, photos, map or model. Observe clarity, effort and who becomes excluded; rotate order if comparing formats. | Conversation or short text is easier; tools require unwanted training, inaccessible movement or equipment; hands-on tasks reduce rather than broaden participation. |
| 8 | Fast feedback is essential. | “After the last time you contacted someone about a problem, what did you expect next? When did you check again?” In a later activity, distinguish acknowledgment, progress explanation and final implementation. | A slower but credible decision is acceptable; a rapid generic acknowledgment fails to sustain engagement; accuracy or ownership matters more than speed. |
| 9 | Transparent explanations make constraints acceptable. | “Tell me about a time an idea of yours could not go ahead. What explanation were you given, and what did you do next?” Later test a concrete constraint and invite participants to challenge it or propose alternatives. | A clear explanation still feels unfair, the constraint is not credible, or participation depends on having an appeal or alternative rather than explanation alone. |
| 10 | Agency is the central unmet need rather than information or another barrier. | Begin with an unprompted recent-journey story: “What would you most want to change, if anything? What have you tried?” Explore responses before mentioning civic participation; distinguish wanting a service fixed from wanting a role in decisions. | Reliable information, safer access, transport service, permission or simply low interest explains the problem better; teenagers want improvements but do not want to help govern them. |

## Fit with the course's Define stage

The course separates observations from explanations and asks for a user-centred problem statement whose need is expressed as a verb and whose wording leaves solutions open. Apply that structure: **interview evidence → interpretation → insight → HMW → follow-up test**.

Suggested methods wording: “We combined desk research with seven semi-structured interviews: six teenagers and one parent. We grouped their accounts into themes and developed insights, a point of view and How Might We questions.”
