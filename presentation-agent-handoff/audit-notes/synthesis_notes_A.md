# Synthesis notes A — three existing AI simulations

**Status:** These are interpretations of fictional AI-generated transcripts, not findings about real teenagers. No hypothesis is validated by this exercise. Ages, schools, journeys, incidents, quoted speech, gestures, interview settings and consent/recording statements are all fictional. References below locate material inside the saved simulations; they are not independent evidence.

## Beatriz — interview_1_beatriz.md

**Persona facts as specified:** 17; 12th-year science student at a public school in Arroios; lives in Olivais with mother, stepfather and 11-year-old brother; metro and walking; student-association and climate-group participation; exam pressure (lines 7–12). The transcript describes giving up volleyball and reducing climate activity because of exams (43, 91).

**Three exact fictional participant excerpts:**

1. “Maybe they didn't lie, maybe the system is just automatic. But it feels like a lie.” — line 79; response to a fictional pavement report marked resolved when the hole allegedly remained.
2. “Because my friends were in it, first. Let's be honest. Then I actually got into it.” — line 95; why she joined the climate group.
3. “Although, ok, contradiction, the council probably has more power?” — line 121; qualification of her initially enthusiastic challenge preference.

**Story-based themes to explore:**

- Feedback credibility: her story of the closed pavement report creates a mismatch between an administrative status and what she sees (75–79). Candidate question: does visible follow-through affect a teenager's willingness to contribute again?
- Existing informal capability: class WhatsApp messages are described as live disruption information; route and carriage choices save time (57–65). Candidate question: what useful mobility knowledge already circulates among peers?
- Social entry, then personal commitment: the simulated account connects joining a cause to friendship before conviction (91–95).
- Everyday autonomy includes negotiation: she conceals a ride-hailing trip because her own safety judgement differs from her mother's rule (49–53). Do not turn either judgement into an objective safety claim.

**Contradictions/counterexamples:** Active civic identity does not guarantee available time (43, 91, 109). She prefers the challenge but acknowledges a council may carry more power (121), so the script does not support “teens reject formal institutions.” She recognises alternative explanations for being passed over by clipboard researchers (83–87), so exclusion by age is her speculation. Her trust story has a narrow single fictional incident, not proof of institution-wide failure.

**Prompt dependence:** The €5,000/six-week offer is introduced by the interviewer at 107. Her wish to count/film crossings (109–113), conditions for believing implementation (115–117), and explicit challenge/council choice (119–121) are reactions to supplied scenarios. The interviewer gives the challenge friends, a finite duration and an appealing activity label, while leaving the council undescribed; this bundles several variables. The street closure proposal follows an explicit temporary-change prompt (123–125). These responses must not be reported as spontaneous solution demand.

## Rodrigo — interview_2_rodrigo.md

**Persona facts as specified:** 15; 10th year in a public school in Marvila; lives with a mother working rotating shifts and a nine-year-old brother; bus-dependent; casual football, gaming and active peer chats; not in formal civic groups (8–13). He sometimes collects his brother after school (97).

**Three exact fictional participant excerpts:**

1. “I don't even know how you report it. Is it an email?” — line 109; after describing a bus-related TikTok.
2. “I don't know how to make a proposal.” — line 165; explaining why he thinks established student representatives would have an advantage.
3. “If my friends go, I go. If it's only me, no.” — line 201; qualification of his challenge preference.

**Story-based themes to explore:**

- Expression without an official route: he describes complaining in chats and posting a video, but does not know how to report formally and expects an empty acknowledgement (99–113). This suggests separating lack of awareness, procedural confidence and low expected impact in real interviews.
- Practical peer organisation: informal bus updates, boarding one stop earlier, a friend-run football table and an online tournament are already in the fictional story (81–89, 129–141). Absence from clubs does not equal absence of social cooperation in this scenario.
- Participation may appear to reward prior institutional knowledge: his concern centres on writing proposals and knowing the process (161–165).
- Routine and caring obligations may constrain attendance independently of motivation (77, 97, 201).

**Contradictions/counterexamples:** He attributes a late test partly to the bus but admits leaving late after gaming (73–77). “Team challenge. Easy.” (193) becomes conditional on friends and timing; he previously abandoned school futsal after two sessions (201). He does not know whether the health centre in his mother's story now exists (189); it cannot even be treated as a settled fact within his fictional account. His closing words combine “no one listens” with not knowing where to go (221), leaving multiple possible barriers rather than validating agency as the sole need.

**Prompt dependence:** The interviewer describes OP Jovem as real funding and asserts peers participate before asking his view (151–159); these assertions are not verified facts. The budget/six-week offer starts at 167. Availability in class, shelter/screen suggestions and filming role follow it (173–181). “Fast” delivery expectations follow the explicit credibility prompt (183–189). Challenge preference and interschool competition emerge after the unequal challenge-versus-meeting comparison (191–201); the latter is his fictional elaboration, not independently elicited demand. The street intervention follows a temporary-change prompt (203–209).

## Mariana — interview_3_mariana.md

**Persona facts as specified:** 14; 9th year at a private school in Restelo; lives around 1.5 km from school; usually driven by her father and collected by a nanny or parent-booked ride; ballet and horse riding; little independent public-transport experience (9–13). She also describes being vice class representative and volunteering with school (119).

**Three exact fictional participant excerpts:**

1. “Because everyone from my class was going. And you can see, like, you finish and there are all the boxes. You did something.” — line 123; on food-bank volunteering.
2. “Like going to a meeting about kids moving around alone and my dad has to drive me.” — line 157; on attending an event away from school.
3. “I want to be allowed to. I don't know if I'd do it every day.” — line 175; qualification of her desire for independence.

**Story-based themes to explore:**

- Participation access may reproduce mobility dependence: an off-site event would require a lift in this scenario (155–157).
- Permission, skills, comfort and infrastructure interact: she describes waiting rather than walking (63–67), nervousness navigating the metro (83–87), and not knowing her pass status (97–101). These leave information and confidence as plausible needs alongside agency.
- Small acts of autonomy: arranging an earlier drop-off and travelling with an older friend's brother are described as workarounds (71–79).
- Social participation plus visible results: food-bank volunteering offers both classmates and a tangible outcome in her account (119–123).

**Contradictions/counterexamples:** She wants the option to travel independently but also enjoys being driven and would not necessarily change daily habits (175). Her mother's concern is not dismissed outright: Mariana herself describes the crossing and pavement as difficult (67). She does not know whether school survey results were communicated and allows that she may have missed the email (115), so “the school never reported back” is not supported even within the fiction. Interviewer notes incorrectly imply she said she had “never” taken public transport alone: the transcript actually says she took the metro once (83). Use the transcript over this note. The off-site access issue arises in response to the interviewer's explicit logistics probe (155), so the note calling it wholly “unprompted” overstates spontaneity.

**Prompt dependence:** Youth-budget/council preferences follow an interviewer explanation (125–135). The €5,000/six-week offer starts at 139; her shift from a drop-off improvement to a safer walking route and preferred roles follows it (141–145). Named deadlines/official visits answer a credibility prompt (147–149). Friends, fear of speaking and perceived boredom appear in the challenge-versus-meeting comparison (151–153). A car-free afternoon follows an explicit temporary-change invitation (159–165). These can inspire neutral follow-up questions but cannot establish real demand.

## Cross-case synthesis limits and useful tests

The same interviewer gives all three scripts an attractive budget scenario and a challenge with friends. Repetition across generated answers is therefore not triangulation. Prefer “the simulations suggest questions about…” over “participants demonstrated…” and avoid percentages or real sample counts.

Useful neutral probes for real interviews: “Tell me about the last time you tried to change something”; “What happened next?”; “How did you learn what the outcome was?”; “What would have prevented you attending?”; “When have you decided an activity was not worth your time?” Test format, peer presence, time requirement and influence separately, allow rejection of every option, and compare stated willingness with a small observable commitment.
