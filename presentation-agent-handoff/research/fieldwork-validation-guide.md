# Fieldwork guide: test the hypotheses without selling the concept

**Purpose:** follow-up research building on the seven interviews and their synthesis.

## Purpose and introduction

Learn what makes teenagers willing and able to influence mobility decisions, including reasons they may not want to participate. Keep the initial discussion independent of the school street lab, funding proposal or team-challenge concept.

Suggested introduction: “We are students studying how people aged 14–18 experience journeys and decisions about streets and transport. We want to understand your own experiences. You can skip any question or stop. There is no expectation that you want to join a project.” Explain what will be recorded and how it will be used; arrange the permissions required by the school/course before recruitment or recording. Do not promise a municipal response or project funding that has not been agreed.

## Teen conversation: 15–20 minutes

1. **Reconstruct a real journey.** “Tell me about your most recent journey to school, from leaving home to arriving. Who decided how you would go?” Follow up on timing, companions and alternatives rather than suggesting problems.
2. **Find an actual incident.** “Was there a moment on a recent journey you wished had gone differently? What happened, and what did you do next?” Allow “nothing comes to mind.”
3. **Understand workarounds.** “Have you changed anything about how you travel because of that? Walk me through it.” Distinguish a one-off response from something they regularly do.
4. **Explore voice without assuming disengagement.** “Have you ever tried to change something about a journey, street or service? Tell me about the last time.” If not: “Have you ever considered it? What happened?”
5. **Trace the response.** If they contacted someone: “Who did you contact? What did you expect? What happened afterwards?” Ask to see an example only if they are comfortable sharing it; do not copy personal details unnecessarily.
6. **Learn from participation elsewhere.** “Tell me about the last activity outside lessons you chose to take part in. What made you start? Did you keep going?” Also ask about something they declined or stopped.
7. **Locate practical constraints.** “If something you wanted to do took place this week, when could it fit? What could prevent you from getting there or taking part?” Let time, transport, access, permission and social discomfort emerge before probing missing areas.
8. **Find existing knowledge.** “If you wanted a change to a journey or street, where would you start looking for help?” Ask about any participation routes they already know before naming programmes.
9. **Check the task's relevance.** “Of the things you would like to change in your day, where do journeys or transport come?” Do not infer desire for civic participation from frustration with a bus.
10. **Invite correction.** “What have I misunderstood? Is there anything you think students doing this project should ask?”

Write down exact wording separately from your interpretation. Record cases that do not fit the emerging themes. Do not treat stated willingness as completed participation.

## Optional concept discussion: separate from discovery

Only after the questions above, introduce any concepts as **hypothetical**. Record these answers separately so a reaction to an attractive proposal does not become evidence of a pre-existing need.

- Compare alternatives with the **same topic, time commitment, peer access and decision power**. For example, compare a short discussion with an equally short visual activity, both feeding into the same decision. The old “council meeting versus challenge with friends” question changed several variables at once.
- Offer “neither” and “another way.” Ask what would make each option unsuitable before asking for a preference.
- Test one variable at a time: a named response owner, timing, accessibility, peer involvement, recognition or funding. Counterbalance the order across conversations where practical.
- Ask: “What would you expect to happen next?” and “What would make you decide not to come?” If a real optional activity is later available, compare intentions with attendance without pressure.
- To test responses to constraints, use a hypothetical example of a rejected proposal with an explanation and a possible next step. Do not tell someone that their actual proposal has been rejected.

## Parent or carer conversation

Ask about the last journey they permitted and the last they declined: what differed, who made the decision, and what practical evidence could change that decision. Ask what attending a school activity would require of the household. Keep their account separate from the teenager's account; one does not establish the other's motivation.

## School and decision-maker conversation

Ask a school contact what time, space and accessible participation routes are actually available. Ask the relevant authority or operator who owns the specific problem and who can answer a youth proposal. Request an example of a previous decision, including a proposal that was rejected. Verify any proposed budget, timetable or implementation promise before designing around it.

## Observation notes

With site permission where required, use one school arrival or departure period to record the site, date, observation window, visible conditions and your vantage point. Note access to stops, crossings and meeting spaces, plus visible workarounds. Do not infer someone's age, disability, motives or willingness to participate from appearance. Observation of traffic can contextualise journeys; it cannot establish why people do or do not participate.

## What to collect next

Start with contrasting real accounts: a regular public-transport commuter, someone dependent on lifts, a teen with little formal participation experience, someone already involved, and a participant whose accessibility requirements might otherwise be missed. Include an 18-year-old, absent from the current interview sample. A few interviews can challenge assumptions and improve the guide; they cannot estimate how common a view is across Lisbon.

For each hypothesis, record: supporting account, contradicting account, alternative explanation, what remains unknown and the next test. Update the problem statement when the evidence changes.
