# Part A — Original desk-research draft (unverified citations)

# Deep Research Report: Youth Participation in Urban Mobility Solutions in Lisbon

## Executive summary

The strongest evidence suggests that the design challenge is **not primarily about persuading teenagers to care about mobility**. Teenagers already experience mobility as a daily issue involving freedom, safety, cost, social life, school access and parental dependence. The deeper problem is that the systems through which Lisbon asks people to influence mobility are largely **adult-designed, formal, slow and weakly connected to the places where 14–18-year-olds already spend their time**. International evidence similarly shows that young people disengage when they cannot see how their contribution will affect a decision, while feeling that one has a genuine say is strongly associated with institutional trust. citeturn684915search0turn684915search1turn684915search2

Lisbon also has an unusually useful starting point. The city already measures school travel through *Mãos ao Ar!*, runs school-mobility programmes, uses school-street-style traffic restrictions through *Mexe-te Pela Tua Cidade!*, operates bicycle trains and mobility education, and has a Municipal Youth Council and participatory mechanisms. Portugal has also made public transport free for young people aged 4–23 and operates a national Youth Participatory Budget open from age 14. citeturn597895search0turn656363search1turn656363search3turn807973search0turn385524search8 The opportunity is therefore less about inventing another participation institution and more about **connecting teenagers to real mobility decisions through mechanisms that are fast, visible, social and consequential**.

The latest published Lisbon school-mobility dataset found that, across all school years in 2023, **43.9% arrived by car, 26.1% on foot, 17.7% by bus, 5.3% by Metro, 2.4% by train and 1.5% by bicycle/scooter/skates/skateboard**. Among secondary-school pupils, the modal order changed: bus represented 29.7%, car 27.0%, walking 22.6%, Metro 11.3%, train 5.0% and bicycle/other small active modes about 2.3%. The survey covered 26,999 pupils but does **not isolate ages 14–18 exactly**, nor does it measure whether journeys are independent or accompanied. citeturn687883view0

Independent mobility is an important hidden variable. A representative Portuguese adolescent study using 2018 HBSC data found 36.5% walked or cycled to school, with active commuting strongly associated with proximity to school and also associated with family support. Earlier Portuguese research found that only 45% of secondary-school children in its sample travelled actively and independently home from school. A 2026 Lisbon analysis further concludes that independence emerges earlier among public-school than private-school pupils and that higher socioeconomic status is associated with less active and public-transport commuting. citeturn860709search0turn860709search9turn860709search2

Youth participation is similarly uneven. In a Portuguese survey of 3,968 pupils, mostly aged 13–18, 1,681 reported weekly civic participation of some kind, but only **259 reported participation in party groups or local political bodies such as municipal assemblies or youth councils**. That suggests a large gap between teenagers' willingness to participate socially and their participation in conventional political structures. citeturn807973search3 Portugal's national Youth Participatory Budget nevertheless showed that real decision power can attract young people: it involved about 15,000 students across 142 municipalities during its first two editions. citeturn807973search11 Cascais provides an especially relevant nearby benchmark: its school-based Youth Participatory Budget has accumulated more than 17,000 participations in 172 sessions over nine editions and has produced implemented school and community projects. citeturn372087search1

The benchmark cases point repeatedly to five design principles. Participation performs better when young people **work on a place they personally use; can make or test something rather than only talk; interact directly with decision-makers; see evidence that something changed; and are reached through schools or familiar social environments rather than expected to discover a municipal consultation themselves**. Braga's school Mobility Labs, for example, led students to present directly to the city's Mobility Advisory Council and resulted in pedestrian crossings and a bus-schedule adjustment; Tartu gave eighth-graders a small implementation budget; Boulder uses walk audits and design activities inside real transport planning processes; and Minecraft-based cases convert abstract planning into something teenagers can manipulate directly. citeturn135947view1turn372087search8turn938039search0turn135947view3

**Research implication.** The most promising design territory is therefore a participation system that gives teenagers a **small but genuine sphere of power over mobility around their school and neighbourhood**, provides rapid feedback, allows visual or physical prototyping, and connects participation to a decision-maker or implementation budget. This is an inference from the evidence above, not a verified statement about Lisbon teenagers; it should be tested directly with 14–18-year-olds before concept selection. citeturn684915search3turn684915search5turn135947view1

## Context and data

### How teenagers travel

The best Lisbon-specific dataset is the Câmara Municipal de Lisboa's *Mãos ao Ar!* survey. It asks pupils from Year 1 through Year 12 how they usually travel to school. In 2023, 135 of 231 schools participated and 26,999 pupils answered, about one third of Lisbon's school population. Because responses are reported by school cycle rather than age, **there is no published modal-share figure specifically for 14–18-year-olds**. Secondary school is the closest proxy for most of the target group, while 14–15-year-olds may also appear in the third-cycle figures. citeturn687883view0

| Mode | All Lisbon pupils, 2023 | Secondary pupils, 2023 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Car | 43.9% | 27.0% |
| Walking | 26.1% | 22.6% |
| Bus | 17.7% | 29.7% |
| Metro | 5.3% | 11.3% |
| Train | 2.4% | 5.0% |
| Bicycle / scooter / skates / skateboard | 1.5% | 2.3% |
| Other modes | remainder | remainder |

Source: CML *Mãos ao Ar! Lisboa 2023*. citeturn687883view0

A striking public/private-school divide exists. Across all years, 34.1% of public-school pupils arrived by car compared with 79.1% in private schools; walking represented 30.5% versus 10.2%, and bus use 21.6% versus 3.6%. CML also reports that car dependence falls with age and that public transport overtakes car use in secondary school. citeturn687883view0turn597895search1 This makes **school type and family socioeconomic context important segmentation variables for interviews** rather than treating Lisbon teenagers as a single mobility population.

Nationally, a representative 2018 HBSC sample of 5,695 Portuguese adolescents with a mean age of 15.5 found 36.5% reporting walking or cycling to school. Living near school more than doubled the odds of active commuting, and family support was positively associated with it. citeturn860709search0 A 2026 Lisbon study using *Mãos ao Ar!* data similarly reports that school type, socioeconomic context and proximity to public-transport stops are associated with travel choices. citeturn860709search2

**Data gap:** neither *Mãos ao Ar!* nor the national adolescent study gives a current Lisbon estimate of how many 14–18-year-olds travel **without an adult**. The most directly relevant Portuguese independent-mobility research located is older: in a study of 1,099 children aged 8–15, only 45% of secondary pupils travelled home from school both actively and independently, although independence increased substantially with age. citeturn860709search9 This gap should be treated as a research question, not filled by extrapolation.

### Safety and autonomy

Portuguese road-safety data does not provide a readily published, current Lisbon breakdown specifically for 14–18-year-olds. The European Commission's 2017–2019 profile for Portuguese road fatalities aged 15–17 found 57% involved motorised two-wheelers, 29% car occupants and 14% pedestrians, highlighting a risk profile different from younger children. citeturn661232search52 More recent national ANSR data shows that vulnerable road users remain a significant safety issue: between 2022 and 2024, roughly 25,000 pedestrians and cyclists of all ages were involved in injury crashes in Portugal, producing 421 deaths and 1,626 serious injuries; most serious pedestrian and cyclist crashes occurred inside urban areas. These figures **cannot be interpreted as teen statistics**, but they establish the safety environment in which independent mobility occurs. citeturn661232search12

Recent Portuguese research reinforces the parental-safety connection. PLANAPP's 2026 *PEGADAS* project found parents identifying inadequate pavements, traffic volume and road danger as leading obstacles to children's autonomy; the research also recommends involving children in urban planning rather than treating mobility purely as an adult decision. citeturn860709search8

### Policies already changing the opportunity space

Portugal's youth public-transport policy is unusually favourable. Under rules updated in 2026, all young people aged 4–18 qualify for the *sub18+TP* free pass and young people through age 23 for *sub23+TP*, regardless of student status, covering applicable collective public transport within metropolitan and intermunicipal systems. citeturn385524search8 This means **price is unlikely to be the central participation challenge for Lisbon's 14–18 group**, although reliability, coverage, safety and parental permission may still matter; that interpretation needs interviewing.

Lisbon operates several school-mobility measures. *Mexe-te Pela Tua Cidade!* temporarily restricts motor traffic at school entrances during arrival or departure periods so that walking, cycling, skating and scooters receive priority; pilots have operated since the 2021/22 school year. citeturn656363search1 *Amarelo*, run with Carris and parish councils, places monitors on selected neighbourhood bus services used by schoolchildren. citeturn656363search6 The municipality also runs bicycle trains and mobility education, while Portugal's IMT has financed school mobility plans and safe-route infrastructure, including €400,000 calls in both 2023 and 2025. citeturn656363search3turn860709search7 Lisbon additionally uses Zone 30 traffic-calming areas. citeturn661232search1

The unresolved question is therefore not whether Lisbon has mobility interventions, but **whether teenagers participate in choosing, designing, testing or evaluating them**. The public information located for these schemes overwhelmingly describes young people as users, pupils or beneficiaries rather than as decision-makers. That is an evidence-based observation from programme descriptions, but not proof that no co-design occurs locally. citeturn656363search1turn656363search3turn656363search4

### Existing channels for participation

Lisbon's Municipal Youth Council is a statutory consultative body intended to bring youth perspectives into municipal policy. Its functions include consultation on municipal youth policy and relevant budget and regulation proposals; membership is structured around youth organisations and representative bodies rather than an open invitation to every teenager. citeturn661232search21turn807973search5 The council was formally reinstalled in April 2025 and has discussed public transport among other youth issues. citeturn661232search2turn661232search30 **No public participation count specifically for 14–18-year-olds in the Lisbon council was located.**

The nationwide Youth Participatory Budget allows anyone aged 14–30 legally resident in Portugal to propose and vote on public investment projects. The mechanism currently has a €500,000 total budget and allows proposals up to €100,000. citeturn807973search0 An evaluation records roughly 15,000 students from 142 municipalities participating in 2017–2018 before the programme's COVID-era interruption. citeturn807973search11

Portugal also has extensive local youth participatory budgeting; a 2023 academic review identified 41 local youth PB schemes, though it explicitly noted the lack of systematic national data on their evolution. citeturn807973search7 Cascais is the clearest Greater Lisbon reference: its Youth PB operates through public schools, allocates €10,000 per school and allows young people to develop larger community proposals; by October 2026 it reported more than 17,000 participations across 172 sessions over nine editions. citeturn372087search0turn372087search1

Student associations and school councils provide another access route, although **no Lisbon-wide dataset was located showing how many secondary-school students participate or whether mobility is regularly addressed through them**. The municipality's education council structure explicitly includes student-association representation. citeturn807973search5

## Barriers to youth participation

The most consistent theme across OECD, EU, UNICEF and academic research is **perceived efficacy**: young people are more likely to participate when they believe somebody with power will listen and something can happen. OECD evidence shows that only 43% of surveyed public youth-policy bodies had involved young people throughout the full policy cycle, even though 92% had informed or consulted them. Youth organisations most frequently identified insufficient political will as a barrier. citeturn684915search0 Across the OECD population, only 32% believe governments are likely to adopt opinions expressed through public consultations, while perceived ability to have a say is strongly associated with trust in local government. citeturn684915search1turn684915search2

The European Commission's consultation for the next EU Youth Strategy, published in 2026 and based on 6,055 responses, found the most frequently selected barriers to participation were **limited opportunity to influence policy (49%)**, insufficient quality civic education (36%), limited political opportunities including voting (30%), inadequate youth-friendly information (29%) and unequal participation access for disadvantaged young people (25%). Respondents prioritised participation in actual EU decision-making, accessible and engaging information, and direct meetings with policymakers as solutions. citeturn684915search4

UNICEF's 2025 *Beyond Being Heard* work, based on intergenerational consultations with 120 young people aged 12–23 from 18 countries, similarly identifies age discrimination, safety, lack of resources and tokenistic participation as constraints, and argues for treating young people as political actors and partners rather than merely consultees. citeturn684915search5 UNICEF's Child Friendly Cities framework therefore requires participation mechanisms to be age-appropriate, accessible and inclusive, explicitly noting that marginalised children are often missed by standard channels. citeturn684915search6

A 2025 scoping review of 48 empirical studies in 24 countries found that local youth-participation programmes often produce positive experiences for participants while achieving much less demonstrable policy influence; outcomes depended strongly on institutional design and gatekeeping. citeturn684915search9 Research on Child Friendly Cities similarly highlights adultism, tokenism, inaccessible processes, governance complexity and underrepresentation of disadvantaged children. citeturn684915search7

Portuguese evidence fits this pattern. In a survey of 3,968 pupils—44% aged 13–15 and 52.4% aged 16–18—weekly civic participation was much more common than involvement in conventional political organisations: 1,681 responses fell into civic activities such as youth organisations, environmental groups, volunteering and online civic initiatives, compared with 259 in party groups or local political institutions. citeturn807973search3 This is especially relevant to the design challenge because it suggests that **the problem may be institutional format rather than complete civic apathy**.

Separate Portuguese research among older youth found lack of political knowledge to be a major reported inhibitor, alongside fear of saying something wrong, expectations that older adults would dismiss their ideas and discomfort with bureaucratic or partisan forms of political engagement. citeturn406629search3 Because that study largely concerns older young people, these mechanisms should be treated as **candidate hypotheses for 14–18-year-olds**, not assumed facts.

Time and convenience matter too. A 2026 English study of public consultation found only 19% of 18–25-year-olds surveyed had participated in one; 76% cited lack of awareness as their principal barrier, ahead of time and topic relevance. citeturn684915search8 This evidence comes from an older age group outside Portugal, but it supports testing whether Lisbon teenagers would ever discover a conventional consultation without school- or peer-based recruitment.

The evidence also suggests several likely motivations among participants: **concrete impact, issue relevance, social belonging, skill development, visibility of results, agency and direct relationships with decision-makers**. Cascais pupils decide over actual budgets; Braga pupils saw crossings and a bus timetable change; Boulder pupils' ideas entered transport plans and physical transit improvements; and Seen and Heard resulted in an actual youth-designed public space. citeturn372087search1turn135947view1turn938039search1turn135947view2

**Working hypothesis for interviews:** teenagers are more likely to intervene when the question is reframed from *“Would you participate in urban planning?”* to something concrete such as *“There is €5,000 and six weeks to change one thing on your route to school—what would you change?”* The evidence supports testing this proposition, but does not establish that Lisbon teenagers will respond in this way.

The cases below deliberately mix **mobility-specific interventions with adjacent public-space and participatory-governance models**, because the latter reveal mechanisms that can be imported into mobility.

Several patterns cut across the cases. **School-based recruitment** reduces awareness and access barriers; Cascais, Tartu, Braga, Dublin and Boulder all use schools as participation infrastructure. citeturn372087search0turn372087search8turn135947view1turn481144search7turn938039search0 **Material agency** matters: budgets, temporary street changes, Minecraft models, street audits and construction give participants something they can manipulate rather than asking them only for opinions. citeturn372087search0turn481144search1turn938039search5 And **feedback loops are critical**: Braga is particularly persuasive because pupils could point to crossings and a bus timetable that changed after their participation. citeturn135947view1

A warning emerges equally clearly: attractive methods do not automatically create power. The Finland Minecraft case demonstrates engagement but limited evidence of planning implementation, while wider academic reviews find that youth participation often produces positive participant experiences without equivalent policy influence. citeturn135947view3turn684915search9 A Lisbon concept therefore needs an explicit answer to **“Who is obligated to respond, by when, and what can actually change?”**

## Users to interview, observe and study online

The sample should deliberately maximise differences rather than approximate the “average teenager”. Conventional convenience sampling from one IST-adjacent school would risk reproducing exactly the participation bias highlighted by OECD and UNICEF, where advantaged young people are more likely to be heard. citeturn684915search0turn684915search6

**Extreme users worth recruiting** include: a 16–18-year-old climate or mobility activist; a student-association leader; a teenager who has never participated in any civic activity; someone driven to school almost every day; a teenager who independently uses bus/Metro/train across several municipalities; a daily walker; a cyclist or scooter user; a pupil attending a private school; a pupil attending a public school; a teenager living in Odivelas, Amadora, Loures, Sintra or another suburb but studying in Lisbon; a teenager whose parents heavily restrict independent travel; someone with a physical, sensory or neurodivergent disability; and a teenager with caring or part-time-work responsibilities that make “attend a meeting at 18:00” unrealistic. The justification is evidence that travel mode and independence vary with school type, distance, socioeconomic context, family support and accessibility. citeturn687883view0turn860709search0turn860709search2turn684915search0

For observation, the highest-value places are likely **school gates immediately before and after classes; a bus or Metro interchange used by several schools; a pedestrian crossing teenagers complain about; a GIRA station near a secondary school; and the first kilometre of selected home–school journeys**. CML's own mobility programmes focus on school fronts precisely because arrival and departure periods concentrate conflicts among cars, pedestrians and active modes. citeturn656363search1 A useful method from Boulder and Nairobi is to give teenagers the camera or audit sheet and have **them** identify what feels safe, inconvenient or absurd, rather than having researchers determine the categories in advance. citeturn938039search0turn938039search5

### Parallel worlds where teenagers already participate

**Gaming and collaborative creation.** Minecraft cases show that teenagers can collaboratively manipulate complex spatial systems when interaction is visual, social and low-risk. citeturn135947view3turn481144search7 Observe guilds, Minecraft servers, Roblox creators or Discord communities for how roles, feedback, visible progress and peer recognition work.

**Sports clubs.** Players voluntarily attend repeated sessions, accept rules, take differentiated roles and work towards visible outcomes. The analogy worth testing is not “gamification” in the superficial points-and-badges sense, but **belonging to a team with a concrete goal and clear feedback**.

**Climate and cause-based activism.** Portuguese research indicates that civic, environmental and volunteering activities attract substantially more teenagers than formal political institutions. citeturn807973search3 Observe how a clear issue identity and moral purpose help young people recruit peers.

**Content creation.** Teen creators repeatedly test ideas against immediate audience reaction. Recent Portuguese mobility communication aimed at Gen Z explicitly uses TikTok, gaming, music, humour and lifestyle rather than bureaucratic messaging. citeturn654005search6turn654005search13 The interesting mechanism is fast feedback and visibility, not simply “put the consultation on TikTok”.

**School clubs and student organising.** These offer existing peer networks, legitimacy and physical meeting spaces. Cascais demonstrates how embedding participation inside schools can reach thousands without asking teenagers to discover a municipal process independently. citeturn372087search0turn372087search1

### Netnography sources

Use online observation as **hypothesis generation**, not representative research. Public posts are strongly self-selecting, and minors require particularly careful ethical treatment.

For Portuguese-language discussion, monitor **r/lisboa and r/portugal**, searching terms such as *transportes públicos, Carris, Metro, Navegante, escola, bicicleta, GIRA, trotinete, segurança, Odivelas, Amadora, Sintra*. These communities contain recurrent firsthand discussions of Lisbon transport, including posts by teenagers themselves; for example, a recent r/lisboa post from a self-described 15-year-old in Odivelas discusses gaining permission to travel independently by public transport. citeturn654005reddit44turn654005reddit53 Do not treat such anecdotes as prevalence evidence.

On TikTok and Instagram, useful exploratory queries include **#mobilidade, #mobilidadeurbana, #mobilidadesustentável, #transportespublicos, #passejovem, #navegante, #metrolisboa, #carris, #gira, #bicicleta, #mobilidadeescolar, #lisboa and #portugal**. These terms are already used in Portuguese mobility communication and youth-facing transport campaigns. citeturn654005search6turn654005search9turn654005search13 English-language comparison searches could include **#schoolstreets, #youthurbanism, #tacticalurbanism, #safestreetstoschool, #youthparticipation, #childfriendlycities, #minecrafturbanplanning and #blockbyblock**.

Useful questions for netnography are not merely “what do teens complain about?” but **what triggers someone to post; what receives peer replies; what language is used for autonomy and safety; which problems are framed as fixable versus inevitable; whether users tag operators or authorities; and what happens after a complaint receives attention**.

## Solution directions

Each direction below is deliberately framed as a **hypothesis to test**, not a recommendation to build immediately.

**Direction: Neighbourhood mobility micro-budget**

**a) The problem it solves.** Teenagers may be asked for opinions without controlling resources or seeing implementation; low perceived influence is a major barrier to youth participation. citeturn684915search4turn684915search9

**b) The user it targets.** Secondary-school pupils who care about local problems but are unlikely to join a formal youth council.

**c) Underlying user need, as a verb.** **Change** something tangible in my everyday environment.

**d) Assumed insight.** *Hypothesis:* teenagers will invest effort when participation carries visible decision power rather than when it feels like an advisory exercise.

**e) HMW question.** **How might we make teenagers feel that their contribution can produce a real change in the places they use every day?**

**f) Riskiest assumptions.** Teenagers care enough about school-area mobility to compete for a budget; €1,000–€10,000 is sufficient to feel consequential; schools will support peer voting; CML or parish councils can commit to implementation; losers will not perceive the process as pointless.

**g) Interview questions / observations to test.**
1. “Imagine your year group had €5,000 to change one thing outside school. Would you want to help decide? Why or why not?”
2. “What would make you believe the municipality would actually build the winning idea?”
3. “Would you rather propose ideas, vote on ideas, help build them, or none of those?”
4. Observe whether pupils spontaneously negotiate trade-offs when given a fixed budget.
5. Test reactions to a six-week implementation promise versus an unspecified future date.

**h) Cases it resembles and learning.** Cascais shows that real budgets embedded in schools can sustain participation over many years; Tartu shows that even €5,000 can allow pupils to implement and measure mobility projects. The lesson is to protect the **decision power**, not merely copy the voting interface. citeturn372087search0turn372087search1turn372087search8

**Direction: Teen street lab**

**a) The problem it solves.** Street changes are normally debated using plans and meetings before teenagers can experience what a different street would feel like.

**b) The user it targets.** Pupils who use a difficult school frontage or crossing but have little interest in formal planning.

**c) Underlying user need, as a verb.** **Try** my idea before adults decide whether it works.

**d) Assumed insight.** *Hypothesis:* teenagers are more willing and more insightful when participation involves building, testing and observing rather than discussing abstractions.

**e) HMW question.** **How might we enable teenagers to explore alternatives to the street conditions they experience every day?**

**f) Riskiest assumptions.** Pupils will volunteer for physical prototyping; temporary changes can legally be implemented; a school street can produce enough observable difference to generate learning; parents and residents will tolerate experimentation.

**g) Interview questions / observations to test.**
1. “If your street could be changed for one afternoon, what would you want to test?”
2. “Would painting, moving objects or measuring traffic feel like participation to you?”
3. “How would you react if adults reversed the experiment afterwards?”
4. Observe which roles pupils choose: designer, builder, photographer, interviewer, traffic counter or presenter.
5. Compare engagement during a discussion-only activity with a hands-on street prototype.

**h) Cases it resembles and learning.** ViaLibera in Palermo found hands-on making and clear task choices important to engagement; Braga achieved real changes after school mobility forums; Clarkson Street turned long-term pupil engagement into a physical school-street intervention. citeturn481144search1turn135947view1turn481144search4

**Direction: Minecraft or game-based Lisbon mobility studio**

**a) The problem it solves.** Conventional maps and planning language may make participation feel technical or adult-owned.

**b) The user it targets.** Teenagers comfortable with gaming, digital creation or visual/spatial thinking, including pupils reluctant to speak publicly.

**c) Underlying user need, as a verb.** **Show** what I mean without having to speak like a planner.

**d) Assumed insight.** *Hypothesis:* familiar creative tools lower intimidation and allow teenagers to externalise spatial ideas more easily than hearings or written submissions.

**e) HMW question.** **How might we help teenagers express complex ideas about places in a form that feels natural to them?**

**f) Riskiest assumptions.** Minecraft is still sufficiently relevant to the target cohort; gaming does not bias participation towards particular genders or socioeconomic groups; city planners can extract actionable information from models; participants do not perceive the exercise as childish; the output genuinely enters a decision process.

**g) Interview questions / observations to test.**
1. “If you wanted to redesign the area outside school, what tool would you naturally use?”
2. Show paper map, Minecraft-like model and street-photo collage; ask which makes expressing an idea easiest.
3. “Would this feel serious enough if a city planner was watching?”
4. Observe whether non-gamers become excluded or whether peers teach one another.
5. Ask participants to explain one design choice to a planner and test whether the digital model improves mutual understanding.

**h) Cases it resembles and learning.** Nairobi's Block by Block projects show digital models can support negotiation and implementation; Dublin involved 139 students and produced 54 Minecraft designs; the Finnish teenage case demonstrates feasibility but provides much weaker evidence of implementation. The key lesson is **game → planner dialogue → decision**, not game → exhibition. citeturn938039search6turn481144search7turn135947view3

**Direction: Teen mobility audit with an official response guarantee**

**a) The problem it solves.** Teenagers see defects and safety problems every day but have no obvious channel that converts their lived experience into accountable municipal action.

**b) The user it targets.** Independent walkers, bus users, cyclists and pupils with disabilities who notice barriers adults may miss.

**c) Underlying user need, as a verb.** **Report and track** what is wrong.

**d) Assumed insight.** *Hypothesis:* teenagers will contribute observations when reporting is quick and they can track whether someone acknowledged, rejected or fixed them.

**e) HMW question.** **How might we make young people's everyday knowledge of the city visible and consequential?**

**f) Riskiest assumptions.** Pupils will repeatedly report problems after initial novelty; municipal teams can respond at useful speed; a “not fixable” response does not destroy trust; location/photo reporting is acceptable for minors; the system avoids turning into an unmoderated complaint feed.

**g) Interview questions / observations to test.**
1. “When you notice a dangerous crossing or broken pavement, what do you do now?”
2. “Would you report it if it took 30 seconds? What would you expect afterwards?”
3. “Which matters more: seeing it fixed, receiving an explanation, or earning recognition?”
4. Walk one route and count how many issues pupils notice without prompting versus with a structured audit.
5. Test whether participants revisit an issue after receiving status updates.

**h) Cases it resembles and learning.** Growing Up Boulder converts youth walk audits into planning evidence; Her Streets combines observation with subsequent redesign. Both suggest that reporting becomes more meaningful when teenagers progress from identifying problems to proposing responses. citeturn938039search0turn938039search5

**Direction: Mobility missions led by student teams**

**a) The problem it solves.** Participation opportunities are often individual, one-off and detached from teenage social life.

**b) The user it targets.** Pupils motivated more by peers, projects and competition than by “civic participation”.

**c) Underlying user need, as a verb.** **Belong and achieve** with people I know.

**d) Assumed insight.** *Hypothesis:* mobility participation becomes more attractive when framed as a team challenge with ownership, roles and a public outcome.

**e) HMW question.** **How might we make improving the city feel like something teenagers do with their peers rather than for an institution?**

**f) Riskiest assumptions.** Competition does not crowd out intrinsic motivation; popular pupils do not dominate teams; schools can allocate project time; missions produce meaningful rather than cosmetic outcomes; quieter teenagers can find valued roles.

**g) Interview questions / observations to test.**
1. “What makes you voluntarily spend time on a group project outside class?”
2. “Would you rather join a mobility council or a four-week team challenge? What makes them feel different?”
3. “Which roles would you want: researcher, video creator, designer, organiser, negotiator, data analyst?”
4. Observe whether friendship groups recruit others or become closed cliques.
5. Test competitive, collaborative and public-showcase framings of the same task.

**h) Cases it resembles and learning.** Tartu turned mobility into pupil-owned projects; Braga developed mobility ambassadors; Portuguese evidence shows civic groups, volunteering and environmental causes attract more adolescent participation than formal local political bodies. citeturn372087search8turn135947view1turn807973search3

**Direction: Teen seat in real mobility decisions**

**a) The problem it solves.** Youth structures can be disconnected from the committees and organisations that actually make transport and street decisions.

**b) The user it targets.** Highly motivated teenagers—student leaders, activists and participants emerging from the broader school mechanisms above.

**c) Underlying user need, as a verb.** **Influence** the people who can actually decide.

**d) Assumed insight.** *Hypothesis:* a small number of highly engaged teenagers want deeper participation, provided it is not symbolic and they are supported to understand technical constraints.

**e) HMW question.** **How might we ensure young people's perspectives remain present when mobility decisions are actually made?**

**f) Riskiest assumptions.** A representative selection method can be devised; officials are willing to share decision space; teenagers can attend meetings scheduled around school; representatives remain connected to a wider peer base; technical language does not exclude them; participation is more than a ceremonial seat.

**g) Interview questions / observations to test.**
1. “Would you sit in a meeting with city officials if you knew a real decision was being made?”
2. “What would make you feel like an equal rather than the ‘young person in the room’?”
3. “Should representatives be elected, randomly selected or recruited through schools?”
4. Observe a mock meeting with one technical constraint introduced halfway through.
5. Ask whether representatives would feel comfortable disagreeing publicly with an adult expert.

**h) Cases it resembles and learning.** Braga pupils presented directly to the Mobility Advisory Council and obtained concrete responses; the Lisbon Municipal Youth Council already provides institutional precedent for youth consultation; OECD and EESC evidence emphasises that meaningful participation requires integration into the policy cycle rather than one-off consultation. citeturn135947view1turn661232search21turn684915search0turn684915search3

Across these six directions, the strongest candidate for a first prototype is probably **a hybrid of the micro-budget, street lab and audit**: one school identifies a mobility problem, pupils gather evidence, several teams create alternatives, the school community chooses one, and CML/parish/transport partners commit in advance either to a small implementation or a publicly explained decision. This is an inference, not a validated solution. Its advantage is that it tests the most important uncertainty—whether **visible decision power changes teenage willingness to participate**—without requiring a new city-wide platform.

## Top 10 assumptions we must verify in interviews

1. **Teenagers distinguish “participating” from simply being surveyed.** We assume that many have been asked questions before but do not consider this genuine intervention; OECD and UNICEF evidence makes this plausible but it has not been verified for Lisbon 14–18-year-olds. citeturn684915search0turn684915search5

2. **Perceived impact matters more than rewards.** We assume seeing a crossing, bus service, bike facility or street change is more motivating than points, prizes or certificates.

3. **Mobility is personally salient enough to compete with school, sport and social life.** Daily exposure does not automatically mean teenagers want to spend free time changing the system.

4. **School is a convenient access channel rather than a credibility problem.** Cascais, Braga and Tartu successfully recruit through schools, but Lisbon teenagers may classify anything school-led as compulsory work. citeturn372087search0turn135947view1turn372087search8

5. **Parents are an important gatekeeper for both mobility behaviour and participation.** Portuguese research links family support to active commuting and parents identify traffic danger as a barrier to children's autonomy, but the exact influence on 14–18-year-olds in Lisbon needs direct investigation. citeturn860709search0turn860709search8

6. **The most disengaged teenagers will participate if the format changes.** Evidence that civic participation exceeds institutional political participation suggests this might be true, but it is possible the same highly engaged minority will dominate any new mechanism. citeturn807973search3

7. **Hands-on and visual formats lower participation barriers.** Minecraft, walk-audit and tactical-urbanism cases support the proposition, but Lisbon teenagers may prefer video, social media, conversation or direct action instead. citeturn135947view3turn938039search0turn481144search1

8. **Fast feedback is essential.** We assume a response within days or weeks will sustain trust, whereas municipal timescales of months or years will destroy engagement; Braga's tangible outcomes and wider research on perceived efficacy make this plausible. citeturn135947view1turn684915search1

9. **Teenagers will accept constraints if adults explain them transparently.** The concept depends on teenagers tolerating “we cannot implement that because…” when the reason is credible rather than interpreting every rejection as proof of tokenism.

10. **The central unmet need is agency, not transport information.** Lisbon already has free youth transport, multiple school-mobility schemes and several participation structures. The project assumes the missing link is meaningful power over decisions, but interviews must remain open to a different conclusion—such as safety, service reliability, parental permission, lack of awareness, social stigma or simply low interest being the dominant barrier. citeturn385524search8turn656363search1turn807973search5

# Synthetic research appendix: from scenarios to provisional HMWs

**Project:** Youth participation in urban mobility, GIDT 2026/27  
**Synthesis date:** 4 November 2026  
**Evidence status:** AI-generated scenarios and interpretations. Not interviews with real people, observed behaviour or validated user insights.

## 1. Purpose and scope

The fixed challenge is: **“How can we encourage youth (aged 14–18) to participate and intervene in urban mobility solutions?”** The focus is young people influencing decisions about journeys, streets and transport. Improving a journey and wanting to help make decisions about it are related but distinct needs.

This appendix completes the synthesis of seven role-play transcripts saved during the earlier session. It explores what could make the existing report's proposed school-based participation concept succeed or fail. Its output is a set of hypotheses, provisional problem statements and research questions. The exercise does not establish what Lisbon teenagers actually think or whether the proposed concept will work.

**Working interpretation:** credibility, practical access and confidence in contributing deserve separate investigation. A teenager could care about a problem, doubt that reporting it helps, and also be unable to attend an activity. An engaging format alone would not resolve all three possibilities.

The accompanying desk-research report remains a separate source category. Its unresolved citation tokens and external factual claims have not been verified in this continuation. Agreement between that report and generated stories is not independent corroboration.

## 2. Tools and methods used

The work comprises review of the supplied desk-research draft; analysis of seven saved AI role-play transcripts; grouping narrative details into themes; separation of scenario content from interpretation; a hypothetical empathy map; and development of provisional insights, a point of view and HMWs. The structure follows the supplied course material's distinction between observations and explanations, then user, need and insight.

The original semi-structured guide covers daily journeys, workarounds, family rules, previous participation, and reactions to hypothetical projects. The six fictional teenagers were constructed to contrast travel modes, civic involvement, school contexts and access needs. A fictional parent adds a stakeholder perspective. **These are seven scenarios, comprising six teen roles and one parent role; they are not seven research participants.**

### Analysis procedure and limits

1. Read the dialogue and identify a specific story, statement or hypothetical reaction.
2. Keep the interpretation separate from what the script actually says.
3. Group related material, retaining qualifications and counterexamples.
4. Express an explanation as a hypothesis, then turn it into a solution-open HMW.
5. Identify what real evidence could support or challenge that explanation.

The fictional interview settings, durations, recordings, consent statements, recruitment details, gestures and interviewer observations are also generated. They are not fieldwork records. No claim about a named institution, street, transport route or service is verified by appearing in a transcript.

The guide introduces **€5,000, six weeks, a winning project and a team challenge with friends**. Positive reactions therefore cannot show that teenagers independently requested these features. The challenge-versus-council comparison changes several things at once, including peers, duration and framing. Repeated generated answers cannot determine which feature matters or how common a preference is. The underlying concept also preceded the scenarios, creating a risk that the fiction simply reproduces its rationale.

### Details that change the interpretation

- **Beatriz:** the script combines friendship as an entry point with later personal commitment. Her initial preference for a challenge is qualified by: “Although, ok, contradiction, the council probably has more power?” An enjoyable format and genuine influence are separate questions.
- **Rodrigo:** “If my friends go, I go. If it's only me, no.” The fictional character's enthusiasm is conditional. Recruiting through friendship groups could also miss isolated pupils, who are not represented in these scripts.
- **Mariana:** “I want to be allowed to. I don't know if I'd do it every day.” An autonomy need does not automatically imply a daily change in travel mode.
- **Tiago:** “I'm a visitor. A visitor who comes every day.” A school-area focus might miss a commuter's most important problem near home. Responsibility across locations must be explored with actual institutions.
- **Inês:** “Those are also my problems. I'm a teenager first.” Her interest in general teen concerns should not be reduced to an accessibility role. Wanting accessible streets and supporting somebody else's bike rack are compatible priorities.
- **Sofia:** “It's not that I don't trust her. I don't trust the street.” The scenario suggests a difference between confidence in a teenager and confidence in the conditions they face. The parent account cannot establish what her daughter wants.

The original interviewer notes sometimes interpret more than the dialogue supports. ## 6. Reframed problem and HMWs

### Provisional point of view

**Teenagers aged 14–18 who notice problems on their journeys to a Lisbon school but seldom use formal participation channels need to contribute to decisions in ways they can access and understand, because they may have useful knowledge while doubting the value—or being unable to meet the demands—of an extra civic commitment.**

This is a working focus for investigation, not a confirmed description of a recruited group. “Journeys to a school” includes connections and home-side barriers, not only the school gate. No particular school has been selected in this exercise.

### Provisional HMW map

| Priority | HMW | Derived from |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | **How might we help teenagers see how their contributions influence decisions about their school journeys?** | I1: consequential contribution |
| Supporting | How might we enable teenagers with limited time or difficult journeys to contribute to mobility decisions? | I2: practical capacity |
| Supporting | How might we help teenagers turn everyday travel knowledge into contributions they feel confident making? | I3: confidence and expression |
| Supporting | How might we enable teenagers who depend on adult permission or support to take part in shaping their journeys? | I4: negotiated independence |

Prioritising the first HMW reflects the direction of the earlier report, **not evidence that trust is the dominant barrier**. Keep the other three as rival or complementary explanations. None prescribes an app, competition, workshop, budget or physical intervention.

The research boundary remains participation in mobility decisions. An HMW solely about making a bus more reliable would move away from that brief unless it explicitly considers how young people can influence the relevant decision.

## 7. Implications for the proposed concept — internal working notes

The earlier report proposed a combination of school-based audit, small budget and temporary street experiment. The scenarios do not validate that combination. They expose questions to answer before choosing it:

| Concept assumption | Unresolved question |
|---|---|
| A school-based activity is convenient. | Can it fit around actual lessons, travel and work without reaching only already active pupils? |
| A team format includes more teens. | Can someone participate without an existing friend group? |
| Walking, filming and hands-on activity lower barriers. | Can everyone access the route, tools and contribution roles? |
| Voting produces a fair priority. | How are essential access needs considered if they are not the most popular proposal? |
| A small budget can create visible change. | What can the responsible institution actually authorise, fund and deliver? No amount or deadline has been established here. |
| An answer maintains trust. | What makes an answer credible, including an explained rejection or a long delivery period? |
| The school frontage is the right scope. | Are the most consequential problems at the gate, on a connection or near home in another municipality? |

Use this section when choosing later experiments. The mid-term outline ends with provisional insights and HMWs; it does not present the concept as a proven answer.

## 8. Validation plan for the original ten assumptions

**All ten remain untested by this simulation.** These are future activities, not completed research. A small qualitative sample can challenge an assumption and reveal differences; it cannot estimate prevalence.

| # | Original assumption | Next test | Disconfirming signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Teens distinguish participation from being surveyed. | Ask about the last time someone requested their view and what happened next, before naming categories. | Giving an opinion is meaningful to them without visible influence, or the distinction does not matter. |
| 2 | Impact matters more than rewards. | Explore a recent voluntary choice; later compare equally specified opportunities with impact, recognition and peer factors separated. | Skills, grades, rewards or social contact explain choices better. |
| 3 | Mobility can compete with other commitments. | Map a real week, ask what an activity would displace, and later observe voluntary uptake. | Frustration with travel does not translate into a feasible commitment. |
| 4 | School is a convenient, credible channel. | Ask how they heard about an activity they actually joined and about previous optional school projects. | School framing feels compulsory or reaches mainly existing student organisers. |
| 5 | Parents gatekeep participation. | Ask separately about a recent permitted and declined journey or activity and who decided. | Permission is routine, or another barrier explains non-attendance. |
| 6 | Changing format reaches disengaged teens. | Establish actual prior involvement, then observe uptake and return at an accessible optional activity. | The same already active pupils participate. |
| 7 | Visual and hands-on formats reduce barriers. | Let people explain the same issue through conversation, text, photos or models; note effort and exclusions. | Simpler conversation works better, or the activity creates access or training barriers. |
| 8 | Fast feedback is essential. | Reconstruct expectations after a real report; distinguish acknowledgement, progress and implementation. | A slower credible answer matters more than a quick generic response. |
| 9 | Transparent constraints are acceptable. | Ask about a real rejected idea, its explanation and what happened next. | Explanation alone is insufficient; fairness, alternatives or appeal matters. |
| 10 | Agency is the central unmet need. | Begin with recent journeys and what they would change before introducing participation. | Reliability, information, safety, permission or low interest better explains the situation. |

Use the [revised fieldwork guide](fieldwork-validation-guide.md) to keep discovery separate from concept reactions. Include teens without established peer networks and an 18-year-old, both missing from the saved profiles. Also verify the relevant school and institutional constraints. Keep contradictory accounts in the synthesis and change the HMW priority if warranted.
