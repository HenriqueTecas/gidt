# Synthetic research appendix: from scenarios to provisional HMWs

**Project:** Youth participation in urban mobility, GIDT 2026/27  
**Synthesis date:** 4 November 2026  
**Evidence status:** AI-generated scenarios and interpretations. Not interviews with real people, observed behaviour or validated user insights.

## 1. Purpose and scope

The fixed challenge is: **“How can we encourage youth (aged 14–18) to participate and intervene in urban mobility solutions?”** The focus is young people influencing decisions about journeys, streets and transport. Improving a journey and wanting to help make decisions about it are related but distinct needs.

This appendix completes the synthesis of seven role-play transcripts saved during the earlier session. It explores what could make the existing report's proposed school-based participation concept succeed or fail. Its output is a set of hypotheses, provisional problem statements and research questions. The exercise does not establish what Lisbon teenagers actually think or whether the proposed concept will work.

**Working interpretation:** credibility, practical access and confidence in contributing deserve separate investigation. A teenager could care about a problem, doubt that reporting it helps, and also be unable to attend an activity. An engaging format alone would not resolve all three possibilities.

The accompanying desk-research report remains a separate source category. Its unresolved citation tokens and external factual claims have not been verified in this continuation. Agreement between that report and generated stories is not independent corroboration.

## 2. Tools and methods used

The work comprises review of the supplied desk-research draft; analysis of seven saved AI role-play transcripts; grouping narrative details into themes; separation of scenario content from interpretation; a hypothetical empathy map; and development of provisional insights, a point of view and HMWs. The structure follows the supplied course material's distinction between observations and explanations, then user, need and insight.

The original semi-structured guide covers daily journeys, workarounds, family rules, previous participation, and reactions to hypothetical projects. The six fictional teenagers were constructed to contrast travel modes, civic involvement, school contexts and access needs. A fictional parent adds a stakeholder perspective. **These are seven scenarios, comprising six teen roles and one parent role; they are not seven research participants.**

### Analysis procedure and limits

1. Read the dialogue and identify a specific story, statement or hypothetical reaction.
2. Keep the interpretation separate from what the script actually says.
3. Group related material, retaining qualifications and counterexamples.
4. Express an explanation as a hypothesis, then turn it into a solution-open HMW.
5. Identify what real evidence could support or challenge that explanation.

The fictional interview settings, durations, recordings, consent statements, recruitment details, gestures and interviewer observations are also generated. They are not fieldwork records. No claim about a named institution, street, transport route or service is verified by appearing in a transcript.

The guide introduces **€5,000, six weeks, a winning project and a team challenge with friends**. Positive reactions therefore cannot show that teenagers independently requested these features. The challenge-versus-council comparison changes several things at once, including peers, duration and framing. Repeated generated answers cannot determine which feature matters or how common a preference is. The underlying concept also preceded the scenarios, creating a risk that the fiction simply reproduces its rationale.

## 3. Most relevant results of the scenario exercise

These are features of the fictional narratives, not empirical findings. All quoted speech below is AI-generated. Source IDs refer to the register above.

| Theme | Content in the scripts | Possible interpretation to test | Qualification or counterexample |
|---|---|---|---|
| **T1. A contribution needs a credible consequence.** | S1 describes a report closed without the expected repair; S6 describes uncertainty about where to send footage. S6: “it feels like writing a letter to nobody.” | Unclear ownership and outcomes may discourage a second contribution. | S7 describes a successful local repair. S5 remains willing to contribute despite disappointment. A rapid acknowledgement is not necessarily a meaningful response. |
| **T2. Interest and availability are different.** | S4 cannot fit activities around commuting and work. S6: “If it's just for fun, I'd say yes and then not go.” | Participation may lose out to practical commitments even when the topic matters. | Grades, friends and timing are competing explanations; this material cannot show which matters most. |
| **T3. Informal competence may not translate into procedural confidence.** | S2 describes peer bus updates and social videos but says: “I don't know how to make a proposal.” | Teenagers may have useful knowledge and skills without identifying with formal participation. | S1 already participates formally and recognises that a council could have more power. Formal formats are not universally unwanted. |
| **T4. Participation can reproduce the mobility barrier it addresses.** | S3 would need a lift to an off-site meeting; S5 questions an activity whose route she cannot access. | A willingness problem may actually be an access or permission problem. | These are distinct barriers, not a single universal need. S3 also values the comfort of being driven. |
| **T5. Wanting an improvement does not imply wanting a project.** | S4: “I don't need an app, I don't need a project. Just stop the bus.” | Teens may want institutions to deliver a service without taking on additional civic work themselves. | This does not imply nobody wants to participate. It challenges making participation the only route to having a need recognised. |

### Details that change the interpretation

- **Beatriz:** the script combines friendship as an entry point with later personal commitment. Her initial preference for a challenge is qualified by: “Although, ok, contradiction, the council probably has more power?” An enjoyable format and genuine influence are separate questions.
- **Rodrigo:** “If my friends go, I go. If it's only me, no.” The fictional character's enthusiasm is conditional. Recruiting through friendship groups could also miss isolated pupils, who are not represented in these scripts.
- **Mariana:** “I want to be allowed to. I don't know if I'd do it every day.” An autonomy need does not automatically imply a daily change in travel mode.
- **Tiago:** “I'm a visitor. A visitor who comes every day.” A school-area focus might miss a commuter's most important problem near home. Responsibility across locations must be explored with actual institutions.
- **Inês:** “Those are also my problems. I'm a teenager first.” Her interest in general teen concerns should not be reduced to an accessibility role. Wanting accessible streets and supporting somebody else's bike rack are compatible priorities.
- **Sofia:** “It's not that I don't trust her. I don't trust the street.” The scenario suggests a difference between confidence in a teenager and confidence in the conditions they face. The parent account cannot establish what her daughter wants.

### Corrections made during synthesis

The original interviewer notes sometimes interpret more than the dialogue supports. Mariana's dialogue says she has taken the metro alone once; the notes' claim that she initially said “never” was not used. Her observation about needing a lift follows an interviewer question about logistics, so it was not treated as wholly spontaneous. Her school-survey story also allows that she may have missed an email; it does not establish that the school never replied. These corrections concern fidelity to the fiction, not verification of real events.

## 4. Hypothetical empathy map

**Focus:** S2, Rodrigo, used as one fictional design lens. This is not a composite of real users or a description of all disengaged teenagers.

| Element | Scenario-based content |
|---|---|
| **Says** | “I don't even know how you report it. Is it an email?” |
| **Does in the story** | Shares informal travel information, discusses problems with friends, posts a bus-related video and joins informal social activities. |
| **Might think — interpretation** | Formal proposals may reward people who already know the process; his contribution may not count. |
| **Might feel — interpretation** | Frustrated by travel problems, uncertain about official procedures, more comfortable contributing alongside familiar peers. |
| **Need to investigate** | Contribute useful knowledge without needing confidence in unfamiliar procedures. |
| **Tension to preserve** | Expressed interest may not produce attendance; friends, timing and other obligations remain relevant. |

## 5. Provisional insights and their tests

Each statement below explains a possible “why.” None is a validated user insight. “Need” is expressed as an action rather than a product.

| ID | Provisional insight | Scenario trace | Need to investigate | What could challenge it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I1** | Teenagers may withhold effort when they cannot tell who will consider their contribution or what happens next, because participation risks becoming another unrewarded task. | T1; S1, S6; qualified by S5 and S7 | **Understand and influence** what happens after contributing | Real users participate despite uncertain outcomes, or awareness and convenience explain decisions better than expected influence. |
| **I2** | A teenager may care but still decline, because travel, study, work and access requirements leave little room for an additional commitment. | T2 and T4; S3, S4, S5, S6 | **Contribute** within practical limits | Participation remains low after practical obstacles are removed, or another concern dominates. |
| **I3** | Teenagers may already hold valuable mobility knowledge but feel unqualified to offer it formally, because the process appears to reward proposal-writing and public speaking over everyday experience. | T3; S2, S6; qualified by S1 | **Express and use** what they already know | People feel confident in the process but are uninterested; simpler expression does not broaden participation. |
| **I4** | Some teenagers' ability to contribute may depend on adults' confidence in the journey and activity, because permission and logistics are negotiated rather than controlled by the teenager alone. | T4; S3 and S7, as separate stories | **Negotiate and exercise** independence | Real teens already decide independently, or time and access explain attendance better than permission. |

T5 is a boundary on all four insights: the project should investigate the value of participation without assuming every teenager wants it. A service improvement is not a reward people should have to earn by joining a project.

## 6. Reframed problem and HMWs

### Provisional point of view

**Teenagers aged 14–18 who notice problems on their journeys to a Lisbon school but seldom use formal participation channels need to contribute to decisions in ways they can access and understand, because they may have useful knowledge while doubting the value—or being unable to meet the demands—of an extra civic commitment.**

This is a working focus for investigation, not a confirmed description of a recruited group. “Journeys to a school” includes connections and home-side barriers, not only the school gate. No particular school has been selected in this exercise.

### Provisional HMW map

| Priority | HMW | Derived from |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | **How might we help teenagers see how their contributions influence decisions about their school journeys?** | I1: consequential contribution |
| Supporting | How might we enable teenagers with limited time or difficult journeys to contribute to mobility decisions? | I2: practical capacity |
| Supporting | How might we help teenagers turn everyday travel knowledge into contributions they feel confident making? | I3: confidence and expression |
| Supporting | How might we enable teenagers who depend on adult permission or support to take part in shaping their journeys? | I4: negotiated independence |

Prioritising the first HMW reflects the direction of the earlier report, **not evidence that trust is the dominant barrier**. Keep the other three as rival or complementary explanations. None prescribes an app, competition, workshop, budget or physical intervention.

The research boundary remains participation in mobility decisions. An HMW solely about making a bus more reliable would move away from that brief unless it explicitly considers how young people can influence the relevant decision.

## 7. Implications for the proposed concept — internal working notes

The earlier report proposed a combination of school-based audit, small budget and temporary street experiment. The scenarios do not validate that combination. They expose questions to answer before choosing it:

| Concept assumption | Unresolved question |
|---|---|
| A school-based activity is convenient. | Can it fit around actual lessons, travel and work without reaching only already active pupils? |
| A team format includes more teens. | Can someone participate without an existing friend group? |
| Walking, filming and hands-on activity lower barriers. | Can everyone access the route, tools and contribution roles? |
| Voting produces a fair priority. | How are essential access needs considered if they are not the most popular proposal? |
| A small budget can create visible change. | What can the responsible institution actually authorise, fund and deliver? No amount or deadline has been established here. |
| An answer maintains trust. | What makes an answer credible, including an explained rejection or a long delivery period? |
| The school frontage is the right scope. | Are the most consequential problems at the gate, on a connection or near home in another municipality? |

Use this section when choosing later experiments. The mid-term outline ends with provisional insights and HMWs; it does not present the concept as a proven answer.

## 8. Validation plan for the original ten assumptions

**All ten remain untested by this simulation.** These are future activities, not completed research. A small qualitative sample can challenge an assumption and reveal differences; it cannot estimate prevalence.

| # | Original assumption | Next test | Disconfirming signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Teens distinguish participation from being surveyed. | Ask about the last time someone requested their view and what happened next, before naming categories. | Giving an opinion is meaningful to them without visible influence, or the distinction does not matter. |
| 2 | Impact matters more than rewards. | Explore a recent voluntary choice; later compare equally specified opportunities with impact, recognition and peer factors separated. | Skills, grades, rewards or social contact explain choices better. |
| 3 | Mobility can compete with other commitments. | Map a real week, ask what an activity would displace, and later observe voluntary uptake. | Frustration with travel does not translate into a feasible commitment. |
| 4 | School is a convenient, credible channel. | Ask how they heard about an activity they actually joined and about previous optional school projects. | School framing feels compulsory or reaches mainly existing student organisers. |
| 5 | Parents gatekeep participation. | Ask separately about a recent permitted and declined journey or activity and who decided. | Permission is routine, or another barrier explains non-attendance. |
| 6 | Changing format reaches disengaged teens. | Establish actual prior involvement, then observe uptake and return at an accessible optional activity. | The same already active pupils participate. |
| 7 | Visual and hands-on formats reduce barriers. | Let people explain the same issue through conversation, text, photos or models; note effort and exclusions. | Simpler conversation works better, or the activity creates access or training barriers. |
| 8 | Fast feedback is essential. | Reconstruct expectations after a real report; distinguish acknowledgement, progress and implementation. | A slower credible answer matters more than a quick generic response. |
| 9 | Transparent constraints are acceptable. | Ask about a real rejected idea, its explanation and what happened next. | Explanation alone is insufficient; fairness, alternatives or appeal matters. |
| 10 | Agency is the central unmet need. | Begin with recent journeys and what they would change before introducing participation. | Reliability, information, safety, permission or low interest better explains the situation. |

Use the [revised fieldwork guide](fieldwork-validation-guide.md) to keep discovery separate from concept reactions. Include teens without established peer networks and an 18-year-old, both missing from the saved profiles. Also verify the relevant school and institutional constraints. Keep contradictory accounts in the synthesis and change the HMW priority if warranted.

## 9. How to describe this work in the mid-term

Suggested methods wording:

> “We reviewed a desk-research draft and used seven AI-generated interview scenarios—six teen roles and one parent role—to explore assumptions. These are not interviews with real participants. The exercise helped us refine our questions and develop provisional insights and HMWs that still need field validation.”

Label each fictional quotation **“AI-generated scenario”** and call the results **“themes from simulated scenarios.”** Avoid “we interviewed seven people,” invented fieldwork dates, population percentages, or statements that the hypothesis was confirmed. Do not claim that a simulation completes the course's user-research component.
