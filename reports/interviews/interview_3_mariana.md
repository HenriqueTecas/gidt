# Interview 3 — Mariana

**Interview 3 — "Mariana"**
**Format:** semi-structured, ~25 min, in-person at a café near Belém (parent present at the next table for the first few minutes, then left)
**Guide:** `interview_guide.md` (questions 1–16 + follow-up probes)

## Participant summary

- **Mariana, 14**, 9th year (9.º ano) at a private school in Restelo.
- Lives in Restelo, about 1.5 km from school. Her father drives her in almost every morning on his way to work. In the afternoon she's picked up by the family's nanny (Dona Fátima) or by an Uber that her parents book.
- Extracurriculars: horse riding (Saturday mornings, Monsanto area) and ballet (twice a week, near Belém).
- Has only taken the metro alone once or twice. Has a Navegante card but barely uses it.
- Polite, says "I don't know" a lot, unsure about the topic, curious about being more independent.

---

## Transcript

**I:** Thanks for doing this. We're students at Técnico doing a project on how teenagers get around Lisbon and whether they have a say in it. There aren't any right or wrong answers, okay?

**P:** Okay. I don't know if I'm going to be very useful, I don't really… go around much. But okay.

**I:** That's useful too, honestly. Let's start easy. Walk me through yesterday. How did you get to school and back?

**P:** So yesterday was Tuesday. My dad took me, like always. We leave at, like, 8:05, 8:10, he drops me at the gate and then he goes to work. He works in Avenida da Liberdade, so it's kind of on the way, but not really, he has to go back down. And then coming back, yesterday I had ballet, so Dona Fátima, she's our… she takes care of my little brother, she picked me up at 5 and took me to ballet, and then my mum got me after.

**I:** And who decided that's how you'd go?

**P:** Um… I don't think anyone *decided*? It's just always been like that. Since I was little. My dad says it's "two minutes", but it's not two minutes, with the traffic on Avenida da Ilha da Madeira it's like fifteen.

**I:** How far is it if you walked?

**P:** Like… twenty minutes? Maybe less. I've walked it, once, when the car had a problem. It's mostly downhill going to school. Coming back is horrible, uphill.

**I:** What's the best and worst part of that trip?

**P:** Best part is my dad lets me choose the music. That's like our thing. Worst part… the queue of cars at the school. Everyone's parents stop on the street with the hazard lights and there's like a security guy whistling and it's really chaotic. Sometimes I just tell my dad to let me out before, at the corner, and I walk the last bit.

**I:** Why do you prefer to walk the last bit?

**P:** Because it's faster, honestly. And… I don't know, it's a bit embarrassing? Like when you're in 9th year and your dad is doing the whole goodbye thing at the gate. *(laughs)* He's nice but. Yeah.

**I:** Where else do you go during the week? Sport, friends, weekends?

**P:** Ballet Tuesdays and Thursdays, that's in Belém, close to the Mosteiro, kind of. Riding on Saturday mornings, that's in Monsanto, my mum takes me because it's far and there's no… I don't even know if there's a bus there. And then friends, mostly at people's houses. Or Colombo, or Amoreiras. Or sometimes we go have crepes in Belém.

**I:** How do you get to Colombo or Amoreiras?

**P:** Someone's mum takes us. Or Uber. My mum has the app and she orders it for me, and I can see it on her phone, like, she sends me the screenshot with the plate number. She always says "check the plate before you get in."

**I:** Have you ever ordered one yourself?

**P:** No, I can't, you have to be 18. Well, I think. My friend Leonor has her own account but it's her mum's card. I don't know how that works.

---

**I:** Tell me about a moment on your way somewhere when you felt unsafe, annoyed or stuck.

**P:** Hmm. Unsafe… not really? Because I'm always with someone. Annoyed, yes, a lot, when Dona Fátima is late because of my brother and I'm at the gate waiting, and everyone has already left, and it's just me and the security guy.

**I:** How long do you wait?

**P:** Sometimes 30 minutes? Once almost an hour, because she was at the doctor with him. And I could have walked home, like, literally, it's 20 minutes, but I'm not allowed. So I just sat there.

**I:** Why aren't you allowed?

**P:** My mum says the road is dangerous. Which, okay, there's that crossing near the roundabout where the cars come really fast and there's no traffic light, just the zebra. And there are parts where the pavement is like… *(gestures)* tiny, and there's cars parked on it. So, I mean, she's not completely wrong? But my friend Inês walks it every day and she lives even further. So.

**I:** Is there anything you do to "hack" or work around a problem — routes, apps, timing, friends?

**P:** *(thinks)* I don't know if this counts. When my mum orders me an Uber to come home from somewhere, I ask the driver to stop at the bakery before my street, not at my door. And I walk the last part.

**I:** Why?

**P:** Because… okay, this is a bit stupid. My mum tracks the Uber on her phone and then she tracks me on Find My. And if the Uber stops at the bakery, she thinks it's just the traffic. And I get, like, five minutes to buy something or just walk. It's the only time I'm alone walking in the street, kind of. *(laughs)* Please don't tell her.

**I:** It stays here. Any other workarounds?

**P:** Um, Carolina — she's my best friend — sometimes she says I'm at her house and then her older brother takes us to Belém on the tram, the 15. So I'm not alone, he's 17. My mum knows I'm with Carolina, she just doesn't know about the tram part. Well, maybe she knows. I don't know.

**I:** So you have taken public transport.

**P:** Yeah, but not *alone*. That's different. Alone, I took the metro once, from Baixa-Chiado to Marquês, to go to the orthodontist, because my dad was stuck at a meeting. And he was on the phone with me the whole time. The whole time. I was so nervous I went the wrong way first — I went to the platform for, like, the other direction. And there was this lady who helped me.

**I:** How did you feel after?

**P:** Honestly? Kind of proud. Like, I told everyone. Which is so stupid because people in my class from other schools do that every day.

---

**I:** What do your parents think about you moving around alone? Has that changed?

**P:** My dad is more relaxed. He says "next year, next year." He's been saying next year since 7th year. My mum is the stressed one. She reads all these news things, like on the WhatsApp of the mums, there's always some story about someone being followed in Belém or a phone getting robbed on the 728. So she gets scared.

**I:** Has anything changed in the last year?

**P:** A bit. Now I can go to the pastelaria near school with friends at lunch. That's new. And she got me the Navegante card in September, she said "for emergencies".

**I:** Have you used it?

**P:** *(laughs)* It's in my drawer. I don't even know if it has money. Like, I think it's the monthly one but I'm not sure if she's paying it every month or if it's only that one time. I should check. I don't know how to check.

---

**I:** When you notice something wrong — a dangerous crossing, a late bus, broken pavement — what do you do?

**P:** Like… nothing? I tell my dad, maybe, if we're in the car. Like "that crossing is dangerous". And he says "yes, it is." *(laughs)* And that's it.

**I:** Has anyone — school, the Câmara, the Junta, Carris — ever asked your opinion about streets or transport?

**P:** No. Never. Well — at school there was a survey, last year I think, about the canteen and the… where you leave the bikes? But nobody has a bike, so. I don't know what happened with that. I think it was for the Eco-Escolas thing.

**I:** Did anything change after the survey?

**P:** I have no idea. I don't think they told us. Maybe they did and I didn't read the email.

**I:** Do you take part in anything outside class — clubs, associations, causes, online communities?

**P:** I'm subdelegada da turma. That's… kind of like vice class rep? It's not a big deal, I mostly just go to one meeting per term. And I did the volunteer thing at the Banco Alimentar with school in December. That was actually nice. And online, I follow a lot of horse accounts. *(laughs)* That's not a cause.

**I:** Why the Banco Alimentar one?

**P:** Because everyone from my class was going. And you can see, like, you finish and there are all the boxes. You did something. Not like a survey where it disappears.

**I:** Have you heard of the Youth Participatory Budget — OP Jovem — or the Municipal Youth Council?

**P:** No. Is that the Câmara? No. Never heard. What is it?

**I:** *(brief explanation: young people propose and vote on projects that the city funds; the council is a body where young people's representatives give opinions to the city)*

**P:** Oh. That's cool, actually. But who goes? Like, how do you even know about it? Nobody at my school talks about that. Maybe the public schools do it more? I don't know. I feel like it's for people who are, like, very into politics. The ones who go to the climate strikes.

**I:** Would you vote in it?

**P:** Maybe. If it was easy. If it was, like, on the phone and someone sent me the link. If I had to go somewhere, probably not, I have ballet. *(laughs)*

---

**I:** Imagine your class had €5,000 and six weeks to change one thing outside your school. Would you want to be involved? How?

**P:** Yes! Yes, actually. I'd fix the drop-off thing. Like, make a place where the cars can stop without everyone blocking the street. Or… actually, no, wait. If the crossing near the roundabout was safe, and there was a proper pavement, maybe more people could walk and there wouldn't be so many cars. Then my mum would let me walk.

**I:** How would you want to be involved?

**P:** I don't know if I'd be good at the, like, building part or the presenting part. I could do the design. I like drawing. Or the Instagram, to get people to vote. I wouldn't want to have to talk to the Câmara people, that's scary. Like adults in suits.

**I:** What would make you believe the Câmara would actually build the winning idea?

**P:** Hmm. If someone from there came to our school and said it to our faces? And said the date. Like, "in March it's going to be done." And then if it's not done, we can go back and complain. Otherwise it's like the survey. You say something and it goes into a void.

**I:** Would you rather join a youth council meeting or a 4-week team challenge with friends? Why?

**P:** The challenge, definitely. With Carolina and Leonor. A meeting sounds so boring, and I wouldn't know anyone, and I'd be scared to say something wrong. With friends you can just say stupid ideas and then one of them is good.

**I:** And if it happened after school — would you be able to get there?

**P:** *(pause)* Oh. That's a good question. Depends where. If it's at school, yes. If it's somewhere in the centre… someone would have to take me. Which is kind of ironic, right? Like going to a meeting about kids moving around alone and my dad has to drive me. *(laughs)*

**I:** If you could test a change on your street for one afternoon, what would you test?

**P:** Close the street in front of school to cars. Just for one afternoon, at pick-up time. And see what happens. Like, do parents park a bit further and walk? Or do they go crazy? *(laughs)* I think some would go crazy. My dad would complain, honestly. He'd say it's ridiculous. But then I want to see if it's actually nicer.

**I:** Would you walk home that day?

**P:** If my mum let me. I'd ask. Maybe if a teacher was there, she'd say yes.

---

**I:** If you could tell the Mayor one thing about being a teenager getting around Lisbon, what would it be?

**P:** *(long pause)* That… we're not allowed to go anywhere because our parents think the streets are dangerous. And maybe they are, a bit. So if you fix the streets, our parents would let us go. It's not that we don't want to.

**I:** Anything we should have asked?

**P:** Um. Maybe ask if we *want* to be independent? Because I do, but also… it's really comfortable being driven. Like when it's raining. I'm not going to lie. *(laughs)* I want to be allowed to. I don't know if I'd do it every day.

**I:** That's really honest, thank you.

**P:** Was that okay? I feel like I said "I don't know" a lot.

## Interviewer notes

- **Key quote on voice:** "Otherwise it's like the survey. You say something and it goes into a void." She contrasted this with the Banco Alimentar volunteering, where "you finish and there are all the boxes. You did something." Visible results seem to matter more to her than being consulted.
- **Says vs does:** she said she has "never" taken public transport alone. Later it came out that she regularly takes tram 15 to Belém with her best friend's 17-year-old brother, without her mum fully knowing. She doesn't count travelling with an older teen as "alone". Her Navegante card has been "in my drawer" since September, and she doesn't know whether it's loaded or how to check.
- **Surprising workaround:** she asks Uber drivers to drop her at the bakery before her street, so that her mum, who tracks both the Uber and her phone (Find My), reads the stop as traffic. That buys her "five minutes" of walking alone. She described it as "the only time I'm alone walking in the street, kind of." This is a small, hidden way of claiming some independence under close parental tracking.
- **Barrier is parental, not personal.** She thinks the parent sees the infrastructure as the problem: the fast roundabout crossing with only a zebra, narrow pavements with cars parked on them, and mums' WhatsApp group stories about the 728. Her Mayor message: "If you fix the streets, our parents would let us go. It's not that we don't want to." Her own answers are mixed, though. She half-agrees the crossing is dangerous, and she admits "it's really comfortable being driven… I don't know if I'd do it every day."
- **The access paradox:** she noticed it herself, unprompted: "going to a meeting about kids moving around alone and my dad has to drive me." Participation events held off-site would leave out teens like her, who are logistically dependent on adults.
- **Format preference:** she strongly preferred a team challenge with friends over a youth council ("adults in suits", "scared to say something wrong"). She would only vote if it were frictionless ("on the phone and someone sent me the link"). She had never heard of OP Jovem or the Youth Council and assumed they're "for people who are very into politics" or "the public schools".
- **Tone/body language:** polite and hesitant at first. She kept apologising ("I don't know if I'm going to be very useful", "I said 'I don't know' a lot"). She got noticeably more animated during the scenario questions, especially the €5,000 idea and closing the street, where she changed her answer mid-sentence. She lowered her voice and laughed nervously when describing the Uber trick ("Please don't tell her").
- **Design opportunity hint:** the school drop-off chaos annoys students too. She already gets out at the corner to avoid it, partly from embarrassment ("your dad doing the whole goodbye thing at the gate"). An afternoon street-closure test at pick-up was her own idea, and she expects some parents, her dad included, to push back.
