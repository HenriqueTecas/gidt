# Interview 5 — Inês

## Participant summary

- **Name / age:** Inês, 16
- **School:** 11th year (science and technology track), public secondary school in Benfica
- **Mobility:** Uses a manual wheelchair (spina bifida). Gets around with Carris buses (ramps are sometimes broken), an adapted-transport service booked in advance, and her mother driving.
- **Traits:** Sharp and direct. Tired of being "consulted" so she can appear in inclusion photos. Knows pavements, kerbs and lifts in detail.
- **Setting:** Café near Colombo, a weekday at about 17:30. About 28 minutes.

---

## Transcript

**I:** Thanks for meeting us. We're students at Técnico doing a project on how teenagers get around Lisbon and whether they have a say in it. There are no right or wrong answers.

**P:** Okay. Just so you know, I said yes because my friend Marta asked me. Not because of the "inclusion" thing. If this is for a poster with me in it, I'm leaving now. *(laughs, but doesn't look like she's fully joking)*

**I:** No poster, promise. No photos.

**P:** Good. Go.

### A. Warm-up / day in the life

**I:** Walk me through yesterday. How did you get to school and back? Who decided how you'd go?

**P:** Yesterday was a mãe day. On Tuesdays she starts work later, so she drives me. We leave at 7:50, it's like eight minutes by car, but then she has to find somewhere to stop. There's the disabled parking spot in front of the school, and there's always a car in it. Always. Yesterday it was a delivery van. So she stops in the bus stop, puts the hazards on, I transfer to the chair, she gets the chair out of the boot... people honk. Every single time.

Coming back I took the bus. I left at 16:40, went to the stop on Estrada de Benfica and waited for one with a ramp that works. The first one, the driver did that thing with his hand, like "sorry". So I waited for the next one. Got home about 17:35. If it all works it should be twenty minutes.

**I:** And who decided?

**P:** It's a negotiation. *(shrugs)* My mother decides the mornings because of her job. Afternoons are mine, more or less, unless it's raining. When it rains she says no bus, and honestly she's right, because the chair in the rain on calçada is... no. The wheels slide, my hands get wet, the gloves get wet. It's not dramatic, it's just gross.

**I:** What's the best and worst part of that trip?

**P:** Best part is the afternoon when it works. I'm on the bus with my headphones and nobody's asking me anything. I like that. That's literally the only time I'm alone all day.

Worst part... there's not one thing. It's the uncertainty. You don't know if the ramp works, if the lift works, if someone parked on the rebaixamento, the dropped kerb. Each thing alone is small. But you're doing maths the whole way.

**I:** Where else do you go during the week? Sport, friends, weekends?

**P:** Swimming, Wednesdays and Saturdays. It's adapted swimming, at a pool... I won't say which, it's like two people in the whole thing, they'd know it's me. My mother takes me because it's evening and there's a hill. Friends, mostly Colombo, because Colombo is flat, has lifts, has accessible toilets. People think we go to Colombo because we're basic. *(laughs)* I mean, also. But it's a mall. It's the most accessible place in Benfica. That's kind of sad, actually.

Weekends, sometimes Baixa with friends on the metro. Depends on the lifts.

### B. Problems, workarounds, feelings

**I:** Tell me about a moment on your way somewhere when you felt unsafe, annoyed or stuck.

**P:** Stuck, literally? Okay. Last year, I think March, I went to Baixa-Chiado with two friends. Going down was fine. Coming back, the lift at the station I needed to get out at, Alto dos Moinhos, was broken. No sign upstairs, nothing on the app. You only find out when you're there. So I'm on the platform, it's 21:00, my friends are standing there like "what do we do". We went one more station and that lift was fine, but then I was in a place I don't know and had to go back up the hill on the street, at night, on bad pavement. My friend Diogo pushed me. I hate being pushed. But I was tired and it was uphill, so.

**I:** Why do you hate being pushed?

**P:** *(pause)* Because then I'm luggage. When I push myself I choose where I go, how fast, when I stop. When someone pushes me they're talking to the person next to them and I'm in front, like a shopping trolley. Also people push badly. They don't see the kerb coming and you go forward. Diogo's fine, he's learned. But strangers grab the handles without asking. That's the worst. An old lady did it once at the bus stop. She wanted to help. I nearly fell out.

**I:** Is there anything you do to "hack" or work around problems? Routes, apps, timing, friends?

**P:** Loads. Okay, one: I go on the road. Not the main road, but in some streets near my house the pavement is so bad, cobbles missing, tree roots, cars parked half on it, that the road is actually safer. Smooth asphalt. I go next to the parked cars. My mother would kill me if she knew. Please don't write that down. *(beat)* No, write it down, it's true.

Two: there's a group chat. It's me, a guy from Telheiras I know from swimming, a woman who's like forty who uses an electric chair, and a few others. We post when a lift is broken. "Jardim Zoológico lift out", photo, done. It's more reliable than the Metro website. Way more.

Three: I know which bus drivers are which. There's one driver on my line in the afternoon, older guy, glasses, who always gets out and pushes the ramp with his foot when it's stuck. It's a manual ramp in some buses, you flip it. If I see it's him I relax. If it's a new driver, sometimes they don't even know how it works.

**I:** Wait, you time your trip around a specific driver?

**P:** Not exactly time it. But if I'm leaving with friends and I know his bus comes at around 16:45, I'll say "let's go now". It's not a system. It's more... luck I try to help a bit.

**I:** What do your parents think about you moving around alone? Has that changed?

**P:** My mother is, mmm, contradictory. She wants me to be independent, she's the one who pushed for me to take the bus alone at fourteen. But then she tracks my location the whole time. If the dot stops for more than five minutes she calls. And if the bus doesn't come, she's the backup, so she's also the one who loses work. She doesn't say it but I know her boss isn't happy. She's done it, like, three times this term.

My father lives in Almada, he's more relaxed. Too relaxed, actually. He doesn't really get the logistics.

It changed in the sense that she got used to it. At the beginning she'd follow the bus by car. Seriously. For like a week. *(laughs)*

### C. Voice and participation

**I:** When you notice something wrong, like a dangerous crossing, late bus or broken pavement, what do you do?

**P:** Honestly? Complain in the group chat. Sometimes I post a story on Instagram and tag Carris. They never answer. Once someone from Carris did reply to a comment, "please send us an email with the details". Who sends an email? I'm sixteen.

There's that app from the Câmara, Na Minha Rua, I think. I used it twice. For a dropped kerb near my building that has a big hole in it. Twice. Then I gave up. I don't know if it says "resolved" somewhere, I never checked. The hole is still there, so.

**I:** Has anyone, like school, Câmara, Junta or Carris, ever asked your opinion about streets or transport? What happened after?

**P:** *(sighs)* Okay, this is my favourite question. Yes. Many times. That's the problem.

In 9th year there was a project with the Junta, about "accessible routes to school". I was the only student in a wheelchair, so obviously I'm the one in it. I did a whole map. Every kerb from my house to school, which ones were dropped, which ones had a car on them, the holes, the bit where the pavement is like forty centimetres wide because of a pole. Photos, everything. Took me weeks. We presented it in the Junta, there was a photographer, I'm in the newsletter, smiling, with the vereador or whatever he was.

And then nothing. Two years. One kerb got fixed, and I think it's because they did roadworks there anyway. Not because of my map.

**I:** How did that feel?

**P:** Like I was decoration. Like they needed a wheelchair in the photo, and I'm a wheelchair. *(pause)* Sorry, that sounds bitter. I am a bit bitter. But I'd do the map again. That's the stupid part. If someone asked me tomorrow, I'd probably say yes, because if I don't, who's going to talk about kerbs? My friends don't even see kerbs. Not because they're bad people. They just don't have to.

**I:** Do you take part in anything outside class? Clubs, associations, causes, online communities? Why that one?

**P:** Swimming, which is a team, kind of. And the group chat, if that counts. *(laughs)* I'm not in an association. My mother wanted me to join a disability association, there's one for spina bifida, but it's mostly parents and it's very... "we're all so brave". Not for me.

I did Parlamento dos Jovens in 10th year. I liked the debating part. I didn't like that the topic was something about mental health and everyone said the same thing for three hours.

Online I follow some disability accounts, mostly English ones, American, British. There's not much in Portuguese that isn't sad or medical.

**I:** Have you heard of the Youth Participatory Budget, OP Jovem, or the Municipal Youth Council? Thoughts?

**P:** OP Jovem, yes, vaguely. Someone at school had an idea for a skate thing, I think? I don't know if it won. Youth Council, no idea. Is that a real thing? Who's in it?

**I:** It's a body where youth associations and student representatives can give opinions to the Câmara.

**P:** So people from associations. So not me. *(shrugs)* I'm not against it. I just don't know anyone in it. And if it's meetings in the Paços do Concelho or wherever at 18:30 on a Tuesday, I'd need to know if the building is accessible, how I get there, how I get home. Most people don't think about that. That's a whole afternoon of planning for one meeting.

### D. Scenarios

**I:** Imagine your year had €5,000 and six weeks to change one thing outside your school. Would you want to be involved? How?

**P:** Okay, first, €5,000 is not much. I looked up once how much a dropped kerb costs and I couldn't find it, but I'm sure it's not cheap, there's drainage and stuff. So maybe two? *(laughs)*

Would I want to be involved... yes, but not as the "accessibility consultant". I'd want to be on the team because I have an idea, not because I'm in a chair. Like, my idea would actually be the disabled parking spot in front of the school. It's always occupied. You don't need money for construction, you need a bollard, or a camera, or the police to come once a week. Or, honestly, paint it bright and put the kids' names on it so people feel guilty. *(laughs)* I'm serious, kind of.

But also, if everybody else in my class wants a bike rack or a bench, I'd vote for that too, depending. I'm not going to make everything about me. That's the thing, I don't want to be the person who always says "but accessibility".

**I:** What would make you believe the Câmara would actually build the winning idea?

**P:** Honestly? A date. Not "it'll be studied". A date, and someone's name, a person, who I can message if it doesn't happen. And show me something they built from last year's. If last year's thing exists, I'll believe it. If it's just a render, no.

And don't take a photo of us when we win. Take a photo when it's built.

**I:** Would you rather join a youth council meeting or a 4-week team challenge with friends? Why?

**P:** Team challenge. Easily. Meetings are people talking. A challenge, you do things.

But, wait, depends where. If the challenge is "go around the neighbourhood and take photos of problems", I can't go everywhere my friends go. Last time we did a field trip for Geografia, there were stairs at the viewpoint and I waited at the bottom with the teacher's bag. *(pause)* So if it's walking around, someone has to think about that before, not on the day.

Also four weeks is fine. Six months, no, I'll have exams.

**I:** If you could test a change on your street for one afternoon, what would you test?

**P:** Ooh. *(thinks)* Take all the cars off the pavement. Just one afternoon. Tow them, block them, whatever. And then film it, me going down the street normally, without zigzagging. And then put them back and film it again. Side by side. People don't get it until they see it.

Or, alternatively, make the mayor do my route in a chair. One afternoon. With the bus. *(laughs)* That's not a street change, I know. But it would change more.

### E. Close

**I:** If you could tell the Mayor one thing about being a teenager getting around Lisbon, what would it be?

**P:** *(long pause)* That I'm not asking for a special thing. I want the same boring thing as everybody else. To leave the house and know I'll arrive. That's it. My friends complain the bus is late. I'd love the bus to just be late.

**I:** Anything we should have asked?

**P:** You didn't ask what I'd change if it wasn't about me. Everybody always asks me about wheelchairs. I also think the buses at night in Benfica are bad for everyone, girls especially, the stops are dark. And there's nowhere to sit near the school that isn't a café where you have to buy something. Those are also my problems. I'm a teenager first.

Oh, and the dropped kerbs, check the slope. Some of them are dropped, but the slope is so steep it's useless, the front wheels hit and you tip. They tick the box in the report. "Rebaixado". Yes, technically.

**I:** Thank you, Inês. That was really helpful.

**P:** Send me what you do with it. Seriously. That's the test.

## Interviewer notes

- **Key verbatim quotes:** "I'd love the bus to just be late." / "Like I was decoration. Like they needed a wheelchair in the photo, and I'm a wheelchair." / "Don't take a photo of us when we win. Take a photo when it's built." / "My friends don't even see kerbs. Not because they're bad people. They just don't have to." / "Send me what you do with it. Seriously. That's the test."
- **Tone and body language:** Guarded at the start (the "poster" comment) and relaxed after hearing there would be no photos. She was quick, dry and funny, and used humour to soften criticism. She went quiet and slower before answering about being pushed and before her message to the Mayor. She apologised for sounding "bitter" and then repeated the point anyway.
- **Ambivalence and contradictions:** She is tired of being consulted, but says she would make the kerb map again "because if I don't, who's going to talk about kerbs?" She wants to join for her ideas, not her chair, yet her first idea is the disabled parking spot. She would also vote for a classmate's bike rack. She says her mother is protective but also pushed for her independence.
- **Surprises:** (1) She deliberately rides on the road beside parked cars because the asphalt is safer than broken pavement. (2) A peer group chat of wheelchair users who report broken metro lifts is, in her view, more reliable than official information. (3) She partly plans her bus around one driver who knows how to free the stuck manual ramp. (4) Some dropped kerbs are "box-ticked" but too steep to use.
- **Barriers to participation that go beyond motivation:** Getting to a meeting is a planning task in itself (building access, getting there and back at night). Field-based challenges leave her out unless the route is checked in advance (example: the Geography trip viewpoint with stairs). Exam season limits long commitments.
- **Credibility conditions for the Câmara:** a concrete date, a named person she can contact, and proof that last year's winning project was actually built. She does not trust renders.
- **Hidden cost:** When transport fails, her mother is the backup and loses work hours (about three times this term), so the problem affects the whole family.
- **Her pushback on our framing:** She asked us not to see her only as a disability case. She raised dark night-time bus stops (a safety concern for girls) and the lack of free places to sit near school as problems for all teenagers. Possible bias: she was recruited through a friend, so she may be more willing to take part than other wheelchair users her age.
