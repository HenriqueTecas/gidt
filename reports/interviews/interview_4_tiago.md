# Interview 4 — Tiago

## Participant summary

- **Name:** Tiago, 16
- **Lives:** Famões, Odivelas municipality, with his mother and younger sister
- **School:** professional/vocational school near Areeiro, Lisbon (restaurant/hospitality course, 2nd year)
- **Commute:** about 1h each way. Carris Metropolitana bus from Famões to Odivelas metro, then the yellow line to Campo Grande, then he changes to the green line or walks, depending on the day
- **Work:** some weekends at a pastelaria near Odivelas station (morning shifts)
- **Traits:** practical, tired, funny, has opinions about every delay pattern. Doesn't trust institutions much, but isn't hostile
- **Interview context:** 22 minutes, after school, sitting on a bench outside Areeiro metro. He had a Compal in his hand and kept checking the time because of his bus connection.

---

## Transcript

**I:** We're students at Técnico doing a project on how teenagers get around Lisbon and whether they have a say in it. There are no right or wrong answers. Is it ok if we record?

**P:** Yeah, fine. Just, I have to go at like ten past, because if I miss the 17:40 at Odivelas the next one is... it's a whole thing.

**I:** Deal. So walk me through yesterday: how did you get to school and back?

**P:** Ok. Alarm at 6:40. I leave home at 7:05, 7:10 max. Walk to the stop, it's like four minutes. Bus to Odivelas metro, that's supposed to be 15 minutes but it's never 15. Then the yellow line to Campo Grande, then I change to the green line to Areeiro. Or if the green line's a mess I just walk from Campo Grande, it's like 20 minutes. Classes start at 8:30. Yesterday I got here 8:25, so a good day.

**I:** And back?

**P:** The opposite but slower. Everyone's going home at the same time. Got home around seven, I think. I fell asleep on the metro and woke up at Senhor Roubado, so that was lucky, it's the stop before Odivelas.

**I:** Who decided you'd go that way?

**P:** No one decided, it's just... the only way. Well, my mom looked at it on Google Maps when I picked this school. She was like "an hour? are you sure?" and I said yes because the course I wanted is here. So I guess I decided. With the course, not with the bus.

**I:** What's the best and worst part of the trip?

**P:** Best part... the metro from Odivelas, because it's the first station so I always get a seat. That's like my 25 minutes. Music, sleep, sometimes I do homework if there's a test.

**I:** And the worst?

**P:** The bus. Easy. The bus in the morning. It's not that it's late, it's that you don't know if it's late. The app says three minutes, then it says seven, then it disappears. Like it died. Then it shows up full and doesn't stop.

**I:** It doesn't stop?

**P:** If it's full it just goes past. Driver does like this [waves hand, palm down]. And you're there like, ok, cool, I'll be late then.

**I:** Where else do you go during the week?

**P:** Not much in the week honestly, I'm tired. Sometimes Odivelas Parque, the shopping centre, with friends, we walk there. Weekends I work at the pastelaria some Saturdays and Sundays, at the station, so that's easy, I take the bus at like 6:30 in the morning. It's empty then, it's actually nice. Sundays the buses are worse though, fewer of them.

**I:** Do you ever go into Lisbon on weekends for fun?

**P:** Sometimes. Cais do Sodré, Bairro Alto, birthdays, things like that. But coming back is the problem. The metro closes around one and then you're in, like, Uber territory. We split an Uber, four people, it's ok. Or someone's dad comes. My mom doesn't drive, so not my mom.

**I:** Tell me about a moment on your way somewhere when you felt unsafe, annoyed or stuck.

**P:** Unsafe, not really. I'm a guy, I'm tall, nobody bothers me. My sister's different, she's 13, she has a whole thing with my mom about it. Annoyed, every day. Stuck...

There was one time, last year, in winter, the bus broke down on the hill, the one going down to Odivelas. Just stopped. Driver says everybody out. It's raining, there's no sidewalk on part of that road, just like a bit of dirt. So you're walking on the road with cars coming. Thirty people walking on the road in the rain. That was, yeah. That was a bit unsafe, actually. I didn't think about it like that then.

**I:** What did you do?

**P:** Walked. What else. Got to school at like 9:40. The teacher didn't even ask. When you say "Metropolitana" they just let you in.

**I:** Really? Why do you think that is?

**P:** Because half the class comes from outside Lisbon. Loures, Odivelas, Amadora, one guy from Vila Franca. Teachers know. It's like a password. [laughs] Ok, I'll be honest, sometimes I use the password when I just woke up late. Not often! But it works.

**I:** Is there anything you do to work around problems? Routes, apps, timing, friends?

**P:** Ah, yes, loads. Ok. First, I don't take the bus at my stop. I walk up one stop, backwards, to the one before. It's like five minutes more walking, but at that stop the bus is still a bit empty, so it actually stops. At my stop it's already full by 7:15.

**I:** So you walk away from where you're going to catch it.

**P:** Yeah, sounds stupid. It's the only thing that works. Second, on the yellow line I always sit in the first car, because at Campo Grande the stairs to change are right there. Saves like two minutes, which is the difference between this train and the next one on the green line.

Third, there's a WhatsApp group, it's like eight of us from my area, different schools. Whoever is at the stop first writes "passou" or "não passou." Or "vem cheio." That's more reliable than the app, honestly.

**I:** Who made the group?

**P:** A girl from my old school, Inês. It was for something else originally, a birthday thing, then it just became the bus group. Nobody planned it.

**I:** And apps?

**P:** Carris Metropolitana app, sometimes Google Maps. Moovit I had, deleted it, too many ads. But like I said, the app is more of a suggestion. I check it, I don't believe it.

**I:** What do your parents think about you moving around alone? Has that changed?

**P:** My mom's fine. She works at a hotel in Saldanha so she also does the metro, she knows what it's like. When I was like 12 she made me send a message when I got there. Now she doesn't care. Well, she cares, she just doesn't ask. My sister she's way more strict with. Like she has to be home before dark in winter. I think that's a bit unfair to her, but I get it.

**I:** Why do you get it?

**P:** The area by the metro at night, the bus stop is kind of dark, there's people drinking near the café. Nothing ever happened, but you know. If I was my mom I'd be the same, probably.

**I:** When you notice something wrong, a dangerous crossing, a late bus, broken pavement, what do you do?

**P:** Complain. To my friends. [laughs] Like, officially? Nothing. What am I going to do?

**I:** Have you ever reported anything, anywhere?

**P:** Hmm. Actually, yeah, once. The Carris Metropolitana thing, the form on the website. When the bus passed me three times in one week. I was so angry I wrote this long thing.

**I:** What happened?

**P:** They sent an email like two or three weeks later. "We thank you for your contact, your situation has been forwarded to the operator." Something like that. The operator! I thought they were the operator. So I don't know who the operator is. Anyway, nothing changed. I didn't do it again.

**I:** Has anyone, school, Câmara, Junta, Carris, ever asked your opinion about streets or transport?

**P:** No. Never.

Wait, actually, at school there was a survey, last year I think, about how we get to school. On the computer, in a class. But I just clicked stuff, everyone did, it was like 30 questions. I don't know what it was for. Nobody said anything after.

**I:** Do you remember who it was from?

**P:** No idea. The school? The ministry? I really don't know.

**I:** Do you take part in anything outside class, clubs, associations, causes, online communities?

**P:** No. I work. I played futsal until last year, at a club in Pontinha, but with work and the commute I stopped. There's no time. If I get home at seven I eat, I shower, that's it.

**I:** Online communities?

**P:** Discord, for games. Does that count? Not a cause, though. Oh, and I follow some pages about the metro on Instagram. There's one that posts when lines are down, and people make memes in the comments. That's my favorite one, the memes are good.

**I:** Have you heard of the Youth Participatory Budget, OP Jovem, or the Municipal Youth Council?

**P:** No. Participatory budget, I heard "orçamento participativo" somewhere. Maybe on a poster? But youth, no. Is it for Lisbon or for Odivelas?

**I:** There are versions in different municipalities.

**P:** That's the thing, see. I live in Odivelas but I spend like twelve hours a day in Lisbon. So which one is mine? If Lisbon does something for young people, it's for people who live in Lisbon, no? I'm a visitor. A visitor who comes every day. [laughs]

**I:** That's interesting. Why do you see yourself as a visitor?

**P:** Because when they say "lisboetas" they don't mean me. And the bus that's bad is the Odivelas bus, not the Lisbon bus. So if I go complain to Lisbon they'll say it's not theirs, and Odivelas... I don't even know where the Câmara de Odivelas is. Honestly. I've never been there.

**I:** Imagine your year had €5,000 and six weeks to change one thing outside your school. Would you want to be involved? How?

**P:** Outside the school here or outside my house?

**I:** Here, outside school.

**P:** Hmm. The crossing by the roundabout, there's a crossing where cars don't stop, everyone just runs. Maybe that. Or benches. There's nowhere to sit while you wait for friends.

Would I want to be involved... depends. If it's during class time, yes, obviously. If it's after school or Saturdays, no, I can't, I work. Not like I don't want to, I can't.

**I:** And how would you want to be involved, if it was in class?

**P:** I'd want to do the actual thing, like build it, not just do the PowerPoint. We do enough PowerPoints. In my course we're in the kitchen half the time, I like doing stuff with my hands. If we had to like paint the crossing or build benches, ok. If it's write a report, I'm going to do the minimum, I'm being honest with you.

**I:** €5,000, is that a lot or a little?

**P:** I have no idea. For a bench? For a crossing? I don't know what anything costs. Is a crossing expensive? It's paint, no?

**I:** What would make you believe the Câmara would actually build the winning idea?

**P:** Nothing, honestly. [laughs] No, ok. If they built it. Like, if they did one before and I could see it. If a school did this last year and there's a bench and it says "made by students from whatever school," then ok, maybe.

Or if there's a date. Like "it'll be done March 15." And then if it's not done, you can go say "hey, it's March 16."

**I:** Would you actually go say it?

**P:** ...Probably not. [laughs] But someone would. Some teacher would.

**I:** Would you rather join a youth council meeting or a four-week team challenge with friends?

**P:** Challenge. Definitely. Council meeting sounds like a meeting. With adults in suits and you sit there and say your opinion and they write it down and that's it.

**I:** What makes the challenge different for you?

**P:** With friends it's fun, you can joke. And it ends. Four weeks, done. A council is like forever, no? You have to keep going. I can't commit to forever. But... it depends when. If the challenge is after school I'm out, same thing as before. I'd pick it if it counts for a grade. That's me being real.

**I:** If you could test a change on your street for one afternoon, what would you test?

**P:** My street in Famões? A shelter at the bus stop. There's just a pole. A pole with a sign. When it rains you stand under the café's awning across the road and then you have to run across when the bus comes. So, put a shelter there, just for an afternoon, see if people use it. They will.

Oh, or a screen. With the real time of the bus. Not the app, a real screen, like at the metro. Even though it'd probably be wrong too. [laughs] But at least I wouldn't have to take my phone out in the rain.

**I:** If you could tell the Mayor one thing about being a teenager getting around Lisbon, what would it be?

**P:** Which mayor? Lisbon or Odivelas? [laughs] Ok, either. I'd say: we spend two hours a day on this. Two hours. Every day. That's like, a part-time job. That's more than I work at the café. And nobody asks if it works. Just make the bus stop when it's supposed to stop. That's it. I don't need an app, I don't need a project. Just stop the bus.

**I:** Anything we should have asked?

**P:** Hmm. Ask about the passe? Like, it's free now for us, which is good, really good, my mom was paying before. But then you've got a free passe for a bus that doesn't come. [pause] And ask the drivers. Some of them are cool, actually. There's one in the morning, older guy, he waits if he sees you running. Everyone knows him. If all of them were like him, half of this conversation wouldn't exist.

Ok, I really have to go now, sorry. If I miss it, it's 25 minutes.

**I:** Go, go. Thank you, Tiago.

**P:** Good luck with the project. Put the bus thing in there, yeah?

## Interviewer notes

- **Strongest quotes:** "I check it, I don't believe it." (about the bus app) / "I'm a visitor. A visitor who comes every day." / "When you say 'Metropolitana' they just let you in. It's like a password." / "Two hours a day. That's more than I work at the café." / "I don't need an app, I don't need a project. Just stop the bus."
- **Tone/body language:** relaxed and joking at first, kept checking his phone for the time. He stopped joking when he described walking on the road in the rain after the breakdown, and he seemed to realize during the interview that it had been dangerous ("I didn't think about it like that then").
- **Surprising workaround:** he walks one stop *backwards*, away from his destination, so the bus still has room and actually stops. He also sits in the first metro car to make the Campo Grande transfer, and a WhatsApp group of about 8 teens from different schools ("passou / não passou / vem cheio") is more trusted than the official app. The group started for a birthday and turned into the bus group without anyone planning it.
- **Says vs does:** first said "nobody ever asked" and "I never do anything officially," then remembered a school survey (he clicked through it at random) and one detailed complaint to Carris Metropolitana. The reply ("forwarded to the operator") confused him and he never reported anything again. He says a public deadline would make him trust the Câmara, then admits he wouldn't personally follow up on it.
- **Jurisdiction gap:** he lives in Odivelas but spends about 12h/day in Lisbon, so he doesn't know which municipality "owns" him or his problem. He has never seen the Câmara de Odivelas. This could matter a lot for any youth-participation design tied to where people live.
- **Practical constraints beat interest:** weekend café shifts and the 1h commute mean anything after school or on Saturdays is a firm no ("not like I don't want to, I can't"). He would join if it happened in class time and/or counted for a grade, and if it involved hands-on building rather than reports or PowerPoints.
- **Knowledge gaps:** had not heard of OP Jovem or the Youth Council and has no sense of what things cost ("Is a crossing expensive? It's paint, no?").
- **Unprompted positive:** the free passe is a big relief for the family, and one older driver who waits for people running for the bus is known by everyone. He thinks driver behaviour matters as much as infrastructure. Worth probing in real interviews.
