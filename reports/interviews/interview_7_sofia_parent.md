# Interview 7 — Sofia

**Interview 7 — Parent / stakeholder**
Format: semi-structured, ~25 min, adapted from the teen guide to a parent's view (child's mobility, autonomy, safety, whether teens should have a say, the €5,000 school project, trust in Câmara/Junta).

## Participant summary

- **Sofia, 46**, nurse at Hospital Santa Maria (rotating shifts, including nights).
- Lives in **Lumiar**. Two children: **Inês, 15** (10th year, secondary school near Alvalade) and **Tomás, 11** (escola básica in Lumiar).
- Drives both kids most mornings on her way to work. Since September this year Inês may take the metro home on her own (Alvalade → Campo Grande → Lumiar).
- Worried about scooters, poorly lit streets and harassment, but says she wants her kids to be independent.
- Member of the school parents' association, but rarely goes to meetings.

---

## Transcript

**I:** Thanks for making time, especially after a shift. As we said, we're students at Técnico working on how teenagers get around Lisbon and whether they get any say in it. There are no right or wrong answers. Can we start with yesterday? How did your kids get to school and back?

**P:** Yesterday... yesterday was a normal day, so the car. I leave at about ten past seven. Tomás first, his school is five minutes away, then I go down to Alvalade to leave Inês, then Santa Maria. It works because it's sort of on the way. Sort of. Some days the Segunda Circular is a car park and I'm late for handover, and my colleagues are not happy with me.

**I:** And the way home?

**P:** Tomás gets picked up by my mother. She lives two streets away. Inês comes home by metro now. Since this school year. She was asking for it the whole of last year.

**I:** Who decided it was time?

**P:** *(laughs)* Officially, me and her father. Really? Her friend Marta's mother. Marta lives near us and they have the same timetable, so the mother called me and said "look, I'm letting Marta go, if they go together it's better, no?" And I said yes before I'd even thought about it. Inês was asking me for a year and I said no, and then another mother calls and in five minutes it's yes. She still throws that at me.

**I:** What's the best and worst part of the arrangement for you?

**P:** Best part: I don't have to leave the ward at five and cross the city. That's huge. Worst part... between about ten past five and quarter to six I look at my phone a lot. We have location sharing, so I see the little dot. I watch it go Campo Grande, then stop, then Lumiar. If the dot stops somewhere strange I get a feeling in my stomach. Usually it's because she went to the Pingo Doce.

**I:** You mentioned the stomach feeling. Can you tell me about a time you were actually worried?

**P:** In October. The dot stopped at Campo Grande for, I don't know, twenty-five minutes, and her phone wasn't answering. I was in the middle of a medication round, I couldn't just leave. Turns out the yellow line had a problem and they were all waiting on the platform, and her phone had 3% battery so she switched it off to "save it for an emergency". *(pause)* Which, I mean... that is exactly what I taught her. So I can't even be angry.

**I:** What were you imagining in those twenty-five minutes?

**P:** Honestly? Not the metro. The metro I trust more or less, there are people, there are cameras. What scares me is the walk from Lumiar station to our street. There is a bit along the Quinta das Conchas side that in winter at six o'clock is already dark, and the lamps, half of them... And there are men who say things. I know because I walk that way myself coming off a night shift. I've had a car slow down next to me. I'm 46 in a nurse's uniform. She is 15. So I'm not imagining things that don't exist.

**I:** Does Inês know that's why you worry?

**P:** I've never told her that, no. I tell her "go by the main road, not through the park". She thinks I'm being exaggerated. Maybe I should tell her. I don't know.

**I:** Besides the dark stretch, what else concerns you on the streets?

**P:** The scooters. The rental ones. They're everywhere on the pavement, people riding them on the pavement, two people on one. At the hospital we see what happens. Faces, wrists. So I said to Inês: no scooters, never. That's the one rule that is not negotiable.

**I:** Has she ever used one?

**P:** *(long pause)* She says no. Her brother told me she did, in the summer, with Marta, in Parque das Nações. She says Tomás is lying. I chose to believe... I chose not to go to war over it. I think at some point you have to choose which wars.

**I:** What about Tomás? Does he go anywhere on his own?

**P:** To my mother's, yes, two streets. And to football, which is close. But in his head he is already as old as Inês. He wants a bike. Here in Lumiar, with the hills and the cars parked on the cycle lane? He'd have to go on the road. No.

**I:** You said you want them to be independent. How does that fit with the rules?

**P:** I know it doesn't sound like it. I grew up in Viseu, I went everywhere alone at ten years old. I want that for them. I really do. I see kids at the hospital, eighteen, nineteen, they can't make a phone call to book an appointment by themselves. I don't want that for Inês. But wanting it and doing it, it's two different things. When it's my daughter and it's dark and the dot stops — the theory goes out the window.

**I:** When you see something wrong on the street, a dangerous crossing, broken lighting, what do you do?

**P:** Complain in the WhatsApp group. *(laughs)* That's the honest answer. The parents' group of Inês's class. Someone posts a photo of a car parked on the crossing, everybody says "absurd", forty messages, and then nothing. Once a father said he'd put it on the Câmara app, Na Minha Rua I think it's called. I don't know if he did.

**I:** Have you ever reported something yourself?

**P:** Once. A lamp on our street that was off for weeks. I called the Junta. And actually they fixed it, I'd say in about two weeks. The Junta is close, you can call and there's a person. The Câmara is a different thing, it's a big machine. You send something to the Câmara and it disappears.

**I:** You're in the parents' association. Has mobility ever come up there?

**P:** I'm a member on paper. I pay the quota. The meetings are at half past six on a Tuesday, and with shifts... I went to one last year. They spent an hour talking about the canteen. Which is important, OK. But no, I never heard anything about how the kids get to school. Maybe they talked about the cars at the gate in the morning, the double-parking. Which is partly me, to be fair. *(laughs)* I'm one of those cars.

**I:** Do you think teenagers should have a say in decisions about streets and transport?

**P:** In principle, yes, of course. They're the ones on the street. Inês knows things about the metro that I don't know — which exits, which carriage is less full, where the group of older boys hangs around. She sees the city at a different hour than me.

**I:** You said "in principle". What's behind that?

**P:** *(pause)* Because I'm thinking — when it's about her own trip, does she have a say? Not really. I decided. Well, Marta's mother decided. *(laughs)* So I'm saying teenagers should be heard by the Câmara, and in my own house I don't really ask. I'm a bit of a hypocrite here, it's true. But it's different, no? The Câmara isn't responsible if something happens to her. I am.

**I:** Imagine Inês's class had €5,000 and six weeks to change one thing outside their school, as a school project. How would you feel about it?

**P:** Honestly, the first thing I'd ask is: is it in class time or extra? Because she has tests, she has English on Saturdays, she has volleyball. If it's extra, on top of everything, I would maybe say no. Not no to the idea. No to her schedule.

**I:** And if it were within school time?

**P:** Then yes, I'd support. I think it's good for them. She would learn more doing that than in half of the subjects. But — and I say this as a parent who has seen a lot of school projects — it has to be real. If they do posters and a presentation and then the posters go in a drawer, that's worse than doing nothing. Because they learn that participating is fake. Inês did a "eco-school" thing in the 7th year, they made a proposal for bike racks, they presented it to the director, and four years later there are no bike racks. She still mentions it. She uses it as an argument against me when I tell her to vote, can you believe it, she's 15 and she already has that attitude.

**I:** What would make you believe the Câmara would actually build what the students picked?

**P:** If it was already decided before they start. Like: "the money is here, it's signed, whatever they choose within these rules, it gets done by this date." And someone with a name, a real person from the Junta, who comes to the school at the start and comes back at the end. Not a letter. And to be honest, €5,000 doesn't build much. A crossing, some lights? But if it's small and it gets done, that's better than something big that never happens.

**I:** Would you be willing to be involved yourself?

**P:** *(laughs)* You're asking the woman who doesn't go to the association meetings. I'd sign whatever needs signing. I'd drive them somewhere if needed, on a day off. But be the parent who organises? No. I don't have it in me right now. I'm being honest.

**I:** If students could test a change on a street near you for one afternoon, what would you want them to test?

**P:** Light. Put some temporary lights on that bit by the Quinta das Conchas and see if people walk there. And the cars at the school gate — close the street for one morning and see what happens. I'd be furious, I'd have to park far away. *(laughs)* But maybe that's the point.

**I:** Last question from our side: if you could tell the Mayor one thing about being the parent of a teenager getting around Lisbon, what would it be?

**P:** That my daughter's freedom depends on a street lamp. That's it. If the walk from the metro was lit and had people around, I'd let her go much more easily. It's not that I don't trust her. I don't trust the street.

**I:** Anything we should have asked?

**P:** Maybe ask the kids what their parents don't know. *(laughs)* Because I'm sure Inês does a lot of things I don't know about. The scooter, probably. And probably other routes. If you want to know how the kids really move, don't ask me — I only know where the dot goes.

## Interviewer notes

- **Key quote, possibly the headline:** "My daughter's freedom depends on a street lamp... It's not that I don't trust her. I don't trust the street." Her fear centres on the last few hundred metres from the metro to home, not on public transport itself.
- **Surprise:** permission for the metro came from a peer parent ("Marta's mother called... in five minutes it's yes"), not from the daughter's year of arguing. Parent-to-parent networks may unlock teen autonomy more than teen advocacy does.
- **Says vs does:** she backs teen participation "in principle" but admits she decides her own daughter's trips: "I'm a bit of a hypocrite here, it's true." She also wants independence while watching the location dot every day ("I only know where the dot goes").
- **Fear drawn from her own life:** her worry about harassment comes from her own night walks home after shifts ("I'm 46 in a nurse's uniform. She is 15."). She has never told her daughter this.
- **Selective blindness:** the scooter ban is "not negotiable", yet she has chosen not to check whether her daughter rides them ("you have to choose which wars").
- **Failed past projects hurt trust:** an old eco-school bike-rack proposal was never built, and her daughter now cites it as a reason not to participate. "If the posters go in a drawer, that's worse than doing nothing."
- **Conditions for the €5,000 project:** must happen in school time (her teen's schedule is full), funding committed up front, a named Junta person who comes at the start and at the end. Trusts the Junta (got a lamp fixed by phone) far more than the Câmara ("a big machine"). Would sign but not organise.
- **Tone:** warm, tired, self-mocking, laughs at her own contradictions (admits she is one of the double-parked cars at the school gate). Thoughtful pauses at questions about rules and honesty.
